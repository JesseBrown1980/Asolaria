Yes, keep this newsletter By Amber Phillips The Trump administration appears to be making moves to release old investigative files on a Democratic congressman, even though he was never charged with a crime. After The Washington Post reported about it this week, Rep. Eric Swalwell, who is running for California governor, threatened legal action if the FBI releases the files. The FBI’s moves have raised alarms within the bureau. And several legal experts say signs point to the Trump administration digging through its vast trove of information to find ways to target political opponents. “This is the autocratic playbook,” said Kimberly Lane Scheppele, a Princeton University professor who studies the fall of democracies, “and clearly weaponization of the FBI in ways that should alarm everyone.” Here’s what’s going on. The FBI looked into Swalwell a decade ago As The Post’s Perry Stein reported this week, the FBI investigated his relationship with a suspected Chinese spy, who appeared to work closely with Swalwell during his 2014 reelection campaign. The Democratic congressman was not accused of any wrongdoing; the Republican-led House Ethics Committee also looked into this and closed its investigation without taking any action. Swalwell’s congressional office said it immediately cut ties with Christine Fang when federal agents informed him in 2015 that they were concerned she might be an intelligence operative. A decade later, Swalwell is an outspoken critic of President Donald Trump and is running in a close race for governor of California, which is one of the most prominent political roles in the nation and a potential launchpad for Democrats with presidential ambitions. Advertisement Trump’s FBI director, Kash Patel, has apparently tried to bring a criminal case against Swalwell, but it hasn’t materialized. The primary is in June, and Patel appears to be making moves to simply release the old FBI investigative files to the public. “The investigatory files are likely to include numerous interviews with Swalwell, his aides, friends and others about the congressman’s interactions with Fang, details about his campaign and more,” Stein reports. It’s almost unheard of to do this There are several rules intended to keep the government from targeting people for political reasons that Patel may be breaching. The FBI needs a reason to reopen an investigation, yet people familiar with the probe told Stein that it is unclear why the bureau reopened its examination of Swalwell. Advertisement Also, agents aren’t supposed to open an investigation of a political figure close to an election unless there’s a really good reason. (Then-FBI Director James B. Comey faced a lot of criticism for reopening an investigation into Hillary Clinton’s private email server just days before the 2016 presidential election, which Trump won.) Evan Gotlob, a federal prosecutor during the first Trump administration, said that once a case is closed, it’s closed. “I’ve worked hundreds of cases that have turned into nothing, and those documents never see the light of day,” he said. “They just go into the ether, and you never think about it again.” So it’s extraordinarily rare for the government to release information from what is essentially an unfinished or closed investigation. “I never, ever released information of an ongoing or closed investigation ever in my career,” Gotlob said. “No matter who it was.” Swalwell is pushing back, threatening legal action Swalwell sent the FBI a cease-and-desist order this week, and his attorneys say the FBI could be violating privacy laws and even the First Amendment by targeting Swalwell for his views about Trump. “Most troubling about this is that we are now literally at war,” Swalwell said in a statement to my colleagues at The Post, referencing the U.S. military campaign against Iran. “We also face threats against the homeland. Kash Patel should be spending every moment trying to keep us safe, not scoring political points. A lot of people have bent the knee to this administration. But I will not, and neither will the people of California.” An FBI spokesperson told my colleagues that the bureau “prepares documents for numerous different reasons, including for release to different agencies and departments to further review investigations that may have been opened under previous administrations.” Setting aside the potential concerns about undermining democratic norms, it’s not the worst thing in the world for Swalwell to be seen as targeted by the Trump administration before a big election. Because incumbent Gavin Newsom is term-limited, California’s gubernatorial race is wide open. There’s a crowded all-party primary, and unless Democratic voters unite on a candidate, it’s possible that only Republicans move on to the general election. (California allows the top two vote getters, regardless of party, to advance.) The Trump administration has targeted Democrats before in ways that boost their profiles. Earlier this year, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth unsuccessfully tried to prosecute Sen. Mark Kelly (Arizona) for a video in which he and other Democratic lawmakers reminded service members that they don’t have to obey illegal orders. A judge ruled that Hegseth had “trampled on Senator Kelly’s First Amendment freedoms.” Now Kelly is a potential presidential contender . He recently told the BBC that he will “seriously consider” running for president. “We’re in some seriously challenging times,” he said. The 5-Minute Fix will be off the rest of this week. The Trump administration appears to be making moves to release old investigative files on a Democratic congressman, even though he was never charged with a crime.

After The Washington Post reported about it this <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/472ba18/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/13/44/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1> week, Rep. Eric Swalwell, who is running for California governor, threatened legal action <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/472e4b2/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/14/44/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1> if the FBI releases the files.

The FBI’s moves have raised alarms within the bureau. And several legal experts say signs point to the Trump administration digging through its vast trove of information to find ways to target political opponents.

“This is the autocratic playbook,” said Kimberly Lane Scheppele, a Princeton University professor who studies the fall of democracies, “and clearly weaponization of the FBI in ways that should alarm everyone.”

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=987489&li=thefix&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1&stpe=default>

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=987489&li=thefix&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1&stpe=default>

Here’s what’s going on.
The FBI looked into Swalwell a decade ago
As The Post’s Perry Stein reported <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/472ba18/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/17/44/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1> this week, the FBI investigated his relationship with a suspected Chinese spy, who appeared to work closely with Swalwell during his 2014 reelection campaign. The Democratic congressman was not accused of any wrongdoing; the Republican-led House Ethics Committee also looked into this and closed its investigation without taking any action.

Swalwell’s congressional office said it immediately cut ties with Christine Fang when federal agents informed him in 2015 that they were concerned she might be an intelligence operative.

A decade later, Swalwell is an outspoken critic of President Donald Trump and is running in a close race for governor of California, which is one of the most prominent political roles in the nation and a potential launchpad for Democrats with presidential ambitions.

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=37351&li=thefix&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1>

 

Trump’s FBI director, Kash Patel, has apparently tried to bring a criminal case against Swalwell, but it hasn’t materialized. The primary is in June, and Patel appears to be making moves to simply release the old FBI investigative files to the public.

“The investigatory files are likely to include numerous interviews with Swalwell, his aides, friends and others about the congressman’s interactions with Fang, details about his campaign and more,” Stein reports.
It’s almost unheard of to do this
There are several rules intended to keep the government from targeting people for political reasons that Patel may be breaching.

The FBI needs a reason to reopen an investigation, yet people familiar with the probe told Stein that it is unclear why the bureau reopened its examination of Swalwell.

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=107214&li=thefix&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1>

 

Also, agents aren’t supposed to open an investigation of a political figure close to an election unless there’s a really good reason. (Then-FBI Director James B. Comey faced a lot of criticism for reopening an investigation into Hillary Clinton’s private email server just days before the 2016 presidential election, which Trump won.)

Evan Gotlob, a federal prosecutor during the first Trump administration, said that once a case is closed, it’s closed.

“I’ve worked hundreds of cases that have turned into nothing, and those documents never see the light of day,” he said. “They just go into the ether, and you never think about it again.”

So it’s extraordinarily rare for the government to release information from what is essentially an unfinished or closed investigation.

“I never, ever released information of an ongoing or closed investigation ever in my career,” Gotlob said. “No matter who it was.”
Swalwell is pushing back, threatening legal action
Swalwell sent the FBI a cease-and-desist order this week, and his attorneys say <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/472e4b2/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/20/44/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1> the FBI could be violating privacy laws and even the First Amendment by targeting Swalwell for his views about Trump.

“Most troubling about this is that we are now literally at war,” Swalwell said in a statement to my colleagues at The Post, referencing the U.S. military campaign against Iran. “We also face threats against the homeland. Kash Patel should be spending every moment trying to keep us safe, not scoring political points. A lot of people have bent the knee to this administration. But I will not, and neither will the people of California.”

An FBI spokesperson told my colleagues that the bureau “prepares documents for numerous different reasons, including for release to different agencies and departments to further review investigations that may have been opened under previous administrations.”

Setting aside the potential concerns about undermining democratic norms, it’s not the worst thing in the world for Swalwell to be seen as targeted by the Trump administration before a big election.

Because incumbent Gavin Newsom is term-limited, California’s gubernatorial race is wide open. There’s a crowded all-party primary, and unless Democratic voters unite on a candidate, it’s possible that only Republicans move on to the general election. (California allows the top two vote getters, regardless of party, to advance.)

The Trump administration has targeted Democrats before in ways that boost their profiles. Earlier this year, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth unsuccessfully tried to prosecute Sen. Mark Kelly (Arizona) for a video in which he and other Democratic lawmakers reminded service members that they don’t have to obey illegal orders. A judge ruled <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/46bf323/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/21/44/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1> that Hegseth had “trampled on Senator Kelly’s First Amendment freedoms.”

Now Kelly is a potential presidential contender <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/46ddc98/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/22/44/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1>. He recently told the BBC <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/46db4fe/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/23/44/69cc27cc0e12064e477e4fc1> that he will “seriously consider” running for president. “We’re in some seriously challenging times,” he said.
