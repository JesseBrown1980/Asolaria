The White House even framed the original initiative as having *“funneled federal resources into radical biotech and biomanufacturing initiatives under the guise of environmental policy”* lawbc.com , which, from the perspective of researchers and entrepreneurs, is a pretty shocking characterization. So this is significant because it essentially halts a coordinated national strategy to grow the biotech sector. Many of us see it as the U.S. stepping away from a leadership role in one of the most important industries of the future. It’s a sharp U-turn, and it introduces a lot of uncertainty for scientists, startups, and investors who were counting on that support. *Jesse:* And how have people reacted to this in the United States? I imagine the biotech community and even some politicians have strong feelings about this sudden policy reversal. What are you hearing from within the U.S.? *Fiona:* The reaction has been one of dismay and frankly deep concern. Interestingly, a number of Republican senators from states like Nebraska, Indiana, Michigan, and others had heavily invested in bioeconomy projects in their states under the Biden-era initiative, hoping to become world leaders in biomanufacturing european-biotechnology.com . In other words, even people in President Trump’s own political camp – in states that stood to gain jobs and industry from biotech – are frustrated because this move undercuts those efforts. The phrase I’ve heard more than once is that this *could backfire*. Industry leaders are warning that the U.S. is at risk of losing its edge. There’s a lot of *“Why are we doing this now?”* being asked. So, inside the country there's pushback and a worry that America is voluntarily surrendering its biotechnology leadership and the economic and strategic advantages that come with it. It’s a rare issue where you have bipartisan concern: everyone from academic researchers to investors to some Republican senators are unsettled by the decision. *Jesse:* You’re the Director of International Outreach at SynBioBeta, so you’re constantly speaking with people around the world. The international response has been swift – almost a mirror image of what we saw in the past. Historically, the U.S. was the magnet for top biotech talent and companies. Starting with Europe – the EU and its member states. How positioned is Europe to take advantage of the U.S. stepping back? Could Europe become the new global leader in biotech and synthetic biology? *Fiona:* Europe is definitely positioning itself to grab the baton. They’ve been eyeing leadership in biotech for a while, and now there’s a sense of *“our time is now.”* There are a few reasons for this. First, Europe has a strong research base and some serious industrial muscle. I’ve spoken with EU policymakers and industry folks who are excited – but also aware that Europe needs to act decisively to capitalize on this moment. But she and others hasten to add that this will *require massive public investment, better scaling infrastructure, and even a bold Europe-wide ‘Biotech Act’* to cut red tape european-biotechnology.com . So Europe knows the opportunity is there, but they also know they must remove some longstanding hurdles (like slow regulatory processes and fragmented markets) to truly lead. On the plus side, we’re already seeing European countries ramp up support. *France has built more than 20 biorefineries* as part of its push to be a world leader in the bio-based economy, and *Italy just approved €2.2 billion through 2027 for new biorefineries* to turn biomass into fuels and materials european-biotechnology.com . That’s real money and real infrastructure going in. Germany might be next – their innovation agency (SPRIND) put out a plan on co-investing in scale-ups for what they call the “deeptech bioeconomy” european-biotechnology.com . And I've heard talk that Germany’s government could channel some of its huge climate fund into biotech projects. So there’s momentum. European scientists and entrepreneurs are also pumped: people like Dr. Guillermo Nevot of the European Synthetic Biology Society are urging Europe to *“take risks and support creative synthetic biology projects across all of Europe if we want a seat in the global arena”* european-biotechnology.com . In summary, Europe sees the opening and is gearing up – politically, financially, and scientifically – to step into a leadership role. If they follow through on these plans, we could indeed see Europe emerging as the powerhouse for biotech innovation in the coming years. *Jesse:* And what about the United Kingdom? The UK isn’t part of the EU anymore, but it has a pretty vibrant biotech and synbio sector of its own. Where does the UK stand in all this? Could London or Cambridge become the next Silicon Valley of biotech, given this moment? *Fiona:* The UK is in a very interesting position. That forward-thinking approach – basically using AI and synthetic biology hand-in-hand to design new organisms and materials – gave the UK a strategic boost. And it’s paying off: the UK has a thriving startup scene in places like Cambridge (often dubbed “the Silicon Fen”) and London, and a lot of talent and venture capital flow there. Moreover, the UK government has been very vocal about turning the country into a “science superpower.” Just recently, they unveiled a *£2 billion, 10-year plan to foster engineering biology* – essentially their national roadmap to boost synthetic biology across medicine, agriculture, and industry european-biotechnology.com . I was at the SynbiTECH conference in London when they announced it, and it was clear the government is throwing its weight behind this field. They’re funding research hubs, streamlining regulations (for example, making it easier to get novel foods like lab-grown meat approved), and generally trying to be the most attractive place to do biotech. So with the U.S. pulling back, UK leaders see a chance to capture some of the talent or business that might have otherwise gone to America. In my conversations with folks in the British biotech community, there’s a mix of determination and excitement – determination to maintain the UK’s status as a leader in synthetic biology, and excitement that they could now play an even bigger role globally. Post-Brexit, the UK is keen to prove itself on the global stage in industries of the future, and biotech is right at the top of that list. So yes, I think we’ll see the UK aggressively court researchers and companies. They’re already doing it, and given their strong base, places like London, Cambridge – even Edinburgh or Oxford – could see a surge of activity if they become the go-to alternative to the U.S. in the eyes of biotech innovators. *Jesse:* And moving across the Atlantic, let’s talk about Canada. That’s a big deal for a country that’s more used to seeing its PhDs and innovators head south. We’re already seeing Canadian institutions advertising their stability and funding to lure researchers who are disenchanted with the U.S. climate. Now, Canada’s also realistic – I saw one analysis pointing out that this opportunity comes at a tricky time, because Canadian universities are under financial strain and don’t have the same scale of funding as the U.S. or Europe ottawalife.com . For example, there’s a proposal floating around to have Canada’s major federal science agencies, like CIHR and NSERC, launch special one-time grant programs explicitly to *attract top U.S. research projects and their teams to Canada* ottawalife.com . Imagine offering a well-funded package for an American synthetic biology lab to relocate to Vancouver or Toronto – that’s the kind of thinking we’re hearing. And Canada has something going for it: immigration policies that favor skilled workers. They already have fast-track visas for tech talent, and I suspect we’ll see those applied to biotech experts too. So yes, Canada might not have shouted from the rooftops yet, but behind the scenes they are absolutely making moves to recruit talent and companies. From Toronto’s biotech scene to the up-and-coming Vancouver area, Canadian leaders see the U.S. retreat as an opening to bolster their own bioeconomy. It could be a very welcome boost for them if they manage to bring some of that talent over. And as someone who deals with the international community, I find folks in Canada are very eager to collaborate and grow – they’re saying, *“bring your projects here, we’re stable and supportive.”* That’s a compelling pitch in uncertain times. *Jesse:* Stepping back, it sounds like the United States is, at least for now, forfeiting its long-held leadership in biotech and synthetic biology. How big are the stakes here? What does it mean for the global biotech landscape if the U.S. cedes ground like this? *Fiona:* The stakes are enormous, frankly. Biotechnology isn’t just another sector – it’s what many of us consider *the* industry of the 21st century, touching everything from healthcare and medicine to agriculture, energy, and environmental sustainability. For the U.S. to step back means they risk not being the place where the next breakthroughs happen or where the next Genentech or Moderna emerges. That’s not only an economic loss – think thousands of jobs and entire new industries slipping away – but also a strategic and intellectual loss. If U.S. leadership wanes, we’ll likely see a more multipolar biotech world, with power centers in Europe, the UK, Canada, and also Asia (let’s not forget countries like China and Singapore, who are heavily investing in biotech regardless of U.S. policy). On one hand, a more distributed global innovation network can be positive – it means more diversity of ideas and possibly more global collaboration. But from an American perspective, it’s quite alarming. The U.S. has traditionally set the pace in biotech, from the early days of genetic engineering to the biotech boom of the 2000s and the mRNA vaccine revolution. Losing that edge could mean the U.S. will have to import innovations or play catch-up on standards and regulations set by others. And there’s a national security angle too: biotech has defense and pandemic response implications. You don’t want to be behind the curve on those. I should note that innovation will go on – science always finds a way – but it may just go on elsewhere if not in the U.S. So globally, we might see a power shift. Europe could become the place to launch bio startups; the UK might set the tone on policy and governance; Canada could host some of the cutting-edge research centers. The U.S. would then be in the backseat for perhaps the first time in the biotech era. That would mark a historic change. However, I will add – the U.S. isn’t out of the game yet. American companies are still world-class, and many U.S. states (like California or Massachusetts) and private investors are still gung-ho on biotech. So the question is whether the federal pullback is a temporary political hiccup or a longer-term shift. If it’s long-term, the loss of U.S. leadership could be hard to reverse. If it’s temporary, maybe the U.S. can regain trust of the community. But make no mistake, every month or year that the U.S. is on the sidelines, others are sprinting ahead. And that changes the balance of who benefits from the next wave of biotech innovation. *Jesse:* Given this new landscape, what can the biotech community do? How should the synthetic biology community — and organizations like SynBioBeta — respond to this kind of policy setback? Yes, policy setbacks like this hurt, especially in the short term, but there’s a lot we can do to keep the momentum going. At SynBioBeta, our perspective is that the community should double down on collaboration and communication. This is a time for the biotech community to rally together across borders. If the U.S. government won’t champion biotech right now, then we as a community will – through private investment, international partnerships, and grassroots advocacy. For example, startups and scientists can start forging more international partnerships so their work isn’t solely dependent on one country’s policy. We’re already seeing U.S. startups opening small labs in Europe or Canada to diversify their footprint. That’s a smart move. SynBioBeta, in particular, is working to connect people globally – it’s literally my job as Director of International Outreach to build those bridges. We’re encouraging dialogue between the U.S. innovators and overseas investors or governments who are eager to help. In practical terms, the community can respond by *sharing success stories and data* with policymakers to make the case that supporting biotech is not a partisan issue but a societal win. We can also support organizations and coalitions that are lobbying for science-positive policies. One thing I’ve been telling colleagues: use the power of our network. The SynBioBeta community is worldwide; if a company in California is facing headwinds, maybe they can find an ally or a grant in, say, Belgium or Singapore to tide them over. The idea is to not let innovation stop at borders. Another aspect is talent: as a community we should make sure young scientists and engineers don’t get discouraged and leave the field. SynBioBeta has always been about celebrating what synthetic biology can do for the world, and that mission doesn’t change just because one government pulls back. In fact, it becomes even more important. So we’re focusing on fostering a sense of unity and purpose. I’ve been heartened by how many people have reached out offering to help bridge gaps – whether it’s investors from Europe looking to fund U.S. projects or academics in one country offering positions to researchers from another. It’s a reminder that this is a global community. So the short answer: we adapt, we collaborate internationally, and we keep pushing forward. The biotech revolution isn’t going to pause for politics. If anything, we come together and make sure progress continues, with or without certain government support. *Jesse:* Finally, Fiona, looking ahead, are you optimistic that the U.S. might course-correct and re-prioritize biotech in the near future? And more broadly, what do you foresee in the next few years for the global synthetic biology field? Are we headed into a new era of international biotech competition, or one of collaboration – or both? *Fiona:* That’s a great question. I’ll answer in two parts. For the U.S., I am cautiously optimistic in the long run. Biotech is just too important for the U.S. to ignore for very long. We may have to get through the current administration’s term, but I suspect there will be strong efforts – from industry, from the science community, even from forward-looking politicians in both parties – to reestablish a national biotech strategy. There’s already talk in D.C. about the economic consequences of losing biotech jobs and investment. So perhaps in a few years, with a different political climate, we could see the U.S. doubling down on biotech again. The hope is that this current rollback is an aberration, not the new normal. I’m optimistic because the U.S. has bounced back before when it realized it was falling behind (I think of the space race or AI research – once there’s a Sputnik moment or a wake-up call, America can mobilize fast). A bit of lost ground now might actually jolt the U.S. system into responding more vigorously later. At least, that’s the hopeful view. Now, for the global synthetic biology field: I see an era of both collaboration and competition, which is healthy. In the next few years, I expect *a more distributed innovation ecosystem*. You’ll have major biotech hubs in multiple countries, not just centered in the Bay Area or Boston. That means more opportunities for collaboration across borders – joint ventures, international research consortia, and cross-border funding of startups. We’re already seeing signs of that. At the same time, let’s be honest, there will be competition – countries will compete to host the best companies, to patent the key technologies, to be first to commercialize new bioproducts. It’s similar to how we see competition in semiconductor or AI industries, but also a lot of global collaboration in research. I actually think both dynamics can drive the field forward. Competition can spur each region to invest more (which is good for everyone in terms of resources available), and collaboration ensures that knowledge spreads and the best ideas get picked up everywhere. What I find exciting is that synthetic biology – the idea of engineering life for beneficial purposes – is becoming a priority not just in one or two countries but worldwide. Europe is moving, the UK, Canada, countries in Asia-Pacific – and the community of scientists and entrepreneurs is inherently international. So I’m optimistic about the field globally. I think we’re entering a renaissance period for biotech innovation. New medicines, sustainable materials, climate solutions – all that will progress. The question is just *where* the center of gravity will be. If the U.S. fixes its course, it will remain a key player among many. If not, others will carry the torch and maybe the U.S. will have to catch up later. Either way, the synthetic biology revolution is not slowing down. As an internationally minded optimist, I’d like to envision a future where the U.S. is back at the table and working alongside Europe, the UK, Canada, and others in a kind of global alliance for biotech, rather than a zero-sum race. But that might be a bit idealistic. In reality, we’ll probably see a mix of collaboration *and* friendly competition. And honestly, so long as the innovations that improve lives keep coming, the world wins in the end. *Jesse:* Fiona, thank you so much for this insightful discussion. Your candid and passionate perspective, grounded in those global conversations you’ve been having, really sheds light on what this policy shift means for everyone. It’s an exciting and uncertain time, but it’s clear the synthetic biology community is not sitting still. Thanks again for joining us. *Fiona:* Thank you, Jesse. It’s been a pleasure. *Jesse:* Stepping back, it sounds like the United States is, at least for

the sidelines, others are sprinting ahead. And that changes the balance of
who benefits from the next wave of biotech innovation.
