The endless resume tweaking, the keyword stuffing, the desperate attempt to be everything to everyone—it’s making you forgettable. This week, we’re flipping the script. I'm not talking about a bad resume. I'm talking about a brilliantly focused one. Instead of a generic resume that's a "maybe" for a hundred different roles, you'll have a resume that's an enthusiastic "YES!" for the one that truly matters. They've become a tired transaction where you awkwardly ask for a job without asking for a job. Instead, I want you to start "anti-networking." This means reaching out to people at your dream companies not to ask for anything, but to offer something. Share a brilliant article you read that's relevant to their work, a tool you discovered that could help their team, or a thoughtful compliment on a recent project they launched. You’re not a job seeker; you’re a valuable peer. Next Steps: - Identify five people in roles you admire at companies you love. - Follow their work. Read what they post, look at their company's recent achievements, and understand their challenges. - Find something genuinely useful or insightful to share with them. This could be an article, a case study, or a resource you think they'd appreciate. - Send a short, no-strings-attached email or LinkedIn message. The goal is to start a conversation, not to ask for a job. It's boring. It's predictable. You know what's interesting? A "failure portfolio." This is a collection of 1-3 projects or initiatives that didn't go as planned. For each one, you’ll write a brief, honest post-mortem: what you tried, why it failed, and, most importantly, what you learned. This demonstrates self-awareness, resilience, and a growth mindset far more powerfully than a list of polished wins. You’re not hiding your missteps; you're showcasing your ability to learn from them. Next Steps: - Identify a project or situation where you took a calculated risk and it didn't pay off. - Write a one-page summary. Structure it with these sections: The Goal, The Plan, What Went Wrong, and What I Learned. - Be honest but professional. Focus on the insights you gained. - You can bring this to an interview or even feature it on your personal website. It's a bold move that will make you unforgettable. Job descriptions are wish lists, not legal documents. Most companies would be thrilled to find someone who meets 60-70% of their criteria and has the passion and intelligence to learn the rest. By only applying for jobs where you tick every single box, you're competing with the largest possible pool of applicants. Instead, I want you to start applying for roles that feel like a stretch. This forces you to focus on your transferable skills and your potential, which is a much more compelling story than just your past experience. Next Steps: - Find a job posting that excites you but where you only meet about two-thirds of the requirements. - Instead of your standard resume, write a "pain letter." This is a cover letter that focuses entirely on the company's problems (that you've researched) and how your specific skills can solve them. - In your application, frame your experience in terms of your ability to learn quickly and adapt. - Even if you get rejected, the process of thinking bigger will change how you see your own capabilities. Instead of just listing "Project Management," try something like: "A belief in radical transparency and agile methodologies to ship projects on time and under budget." This tells a recruiter not just what you can do, but how you think. It will repel companies that don't share your values and attract the ones that do. Next Steps: - Look at your current skills section. For each skill, ask yourself, "What is my philosophy about this? What do I believe?" - Rewrite each skill as a short statement of belief or a "point of view." - Focus on the 3-4 most important skills for your target role and give them this treatment. - This transforms your resume from a passive list of qualifications into an active and engaging professional manifesto.
