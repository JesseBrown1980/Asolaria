Voisin/The Washington Post) Spring break is coming up — and so are the costs. Travelers are facing a double whammy this spring travel season. The Department of Homeland Security remains shut down, leaving Transportation Security Administration workers without pay, and fuel prices are going up amid the war in Iran. Lines are long at airports, and airfares are rising along with energy prices. The dynamic is creating political pressure for both the White House and Congress. Testimonials of extraordinary waits in airport security lines are going viral as a growing number of TSA workers either resign or stop showing up for work. This is the third shutdown TSA officers have had to endure in the past five months, and officers are feeling the financial pitch more acutely each day. “People just can’t come to work because they don’t have the funds,” said Johnny Jones, treasurer of AFGE TSA Council 100, which represents the agency’s staff. TSA employees missed their first full paycheck last Friday, and more than 300 employees have quit, according to DHS. “I anticipate that number exploding in the next few days.” Meanwhile, fuel prices for drivers and fliers are starting to spike following Iran’s closure of the Strait of Hormuz. Iran closed the waterway, through which 20 percent of the world’s oil traffic passes, in response to the U.S.-Israeli military offensive. Energy analysts have long considered its closure an economic doomsday scenario. Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, broke $117 a barrel yesterday, the same day the national average for a gallon of gas hit $3.88. Airfares are also spiking , with airlines implementing fees to weather forecasts for higher fuel costs. It can take weeks for the full impact of higher crude oil prices to affect consumer fuels, so gas and jet fuel prices could spike even higher. Advertisement High energy prices were one of the principal attack points Republicans levied against Democrats during the Biden administration, tying them to Democrats’ ambitious climate agenda. The reality was a bit more complex — a mixture of travelers emerging from the pandemic, reluctance from oil majors to invest in new drilling and, of course, the war in Ukraine that led to sanctions on Russian energy — but Democrats still suffered at the polls. Republicans could, too. The war in Iran remains controversial, with 40 percent of respondents to a Washington Post poll last week opposing the U.S. military campaign. The conflict has a more direct, and swift, impact on fuel prices than the Biden administration’s climate agenda. The Trump administration seems aware of this liability. President Donald Trump has been pleading with U.S. allies to help restore traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. If the strait remains closed, ships could back up, storage capacity in oil-producing Gulf states could max out and oil production itself could cease. It would take weeks or months in that case to get oil pumping out of the ground again once traffic is restored through the strait. The Trump administration is considering an array of actions to cool the price of oil, including lifting sanctions on Iranian oil that’s already out for export. (Mind you, doing so would put money back in the coffers of the Iranian regime that is conducting retaliatory strikes on U.S. and allied targets). The administration already lifted sanctions on some Russian oil last week in a bid to add more supply to the global market. Rachel Chason, Evan Halper, Victoria Craw, Siham Shamalakh and Tara Copp have more . Trump also tried to rein in Israel’s attacks on the South Pars gas field — a major natural gas field split between Qatari and Iranian control — as energy markets face increasing instability. Advertisement “I told him don’t do that,” Trump told reporters yesterday, apparently referring to Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. On the TSA front, Congress still appears a long way from reopening the Department of Homeland Security, which has been shut down since mid-February. Democrats refuse to fund the department unless Congress passes measures to temper federal immigration enforcement efforts. Republicans are advocating a DHS funding bill that the House passed earlier this month. The White House and Senate Democrats have traded offers, but Democrats are insistent on their demands. Democrats have pitched funding TSA and other non-immigration related aspects of the department to allow workers to collect their paychecks without giving up their fight on immigration enforcement. Republicans have refused. “The only question Republicans must answer today is this: Has enough been enough?” Senate Minority Leader Charles E. Schumer (D-New York) said on the Senate floor yesterday. "Will they finally fund TSA or keep holding it and our travelers hostage?” Congress is set to head into its own spring break the week after next, when members will fly home and spend time in their districts. Everett B. Kelley, president of the AFGE union, wrote to lawmakers yesterday urging them to cancel their recess until Congress can find a funding measure to pay TSA workers. Kelley wrote that DHS staffers are turning to selling plasma and visiting food banks to make ends meet and that an extended shutdown would jeopardize national security. Senate Majority Leader John Thune (R-South Dakota) said he would keep the chamber in town if the shutdown continues beyond next week. “I can’t see us taking a break if the government’s still shut down,” he said. So far, European leaders have been dismissive of sending their navies to the strait while it’s in open conflict, though several European nations entertained diplomatic assistance or military help when fighting subsides. There’s a lot of frustration across the Atlantic with the situation Trump instigated by bombing Iran. Takaichi is in a challenging position, and she admitted as much to the Japanese parliament before going to Washington, saying she was bracing for a “very difficult” meeting. Japan is heavily reliant on oil that travels through the Strait of Hormuz, but the country’s post-war constitution has strict limitations on use of its military. The Japanese public also largely disapproves of getting militarily involved with the war, but Japan is reliant on the U.S. for defense. “I expect Japan to step up, you know, because we have that kind of relationship,” Trump said. Spring break is coming up — and so are the costs.

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=906529&li=powerup&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69bd1f2d65c20309a123a238&stpe=default>

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=906529&li=powerup&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69bd1f2d65c20309a123a238&stpe=default>

Travelers are facing a double whammy this spring travel season. The Department of Homeland Security remains shut down, leaving Transportation Security Administration workers without pay, and fuel prices are going up amid the war in Iran. Lines are long at airports, and airfares are rising along with energy prices.

The dynamic is creating political pressure for both the White House and Congress. Testimonials of extraordinary waits in airport security lines are going viral as a growing number of TSA workers either resign or stop showing up for work. This is the third shutdown TSA officers have had to endure in the past five months, and officers are feeling the financial pitch more acutely each day.

“People just can’t come to work because they don’t have the funds,” said Johnny Jones, treasurer of AFGE TSA Council 100, which represents the agency’s staff. TSA employees missed their first full paycheck last Friday, and more than 300 employees have quit, according to DHS. “I anticipate that number exploding in the next few days.”

Meanwhile, fuel prices for drivers and fliers are starting to spike following Iran’s closure of the Strait of Hormuz. Iran closed the waterway, through which 20 percent of the world’s oil traffic passes, in response to the U.S.-Israeli military offensive. Energy analysts have long considered its closure an economic doomsday scenario.

Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, broke $117 a barrel yesterday, the same day the national average for a gallon of gas hit $3.88. Airfares are also spiking <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/car-ln-tr/470ef31/69bd1f2d65c20309a123a238/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/20/57/69bd1f2d65c20309a123a238>, with airlines implementing fees to weather forecasts for higher fuel costs. It can take weeks for the full impact of higher crude oil prices to affect consumer fuels, so gas and jet fuel prices could spike even higher.

High energy prices were one of the principal attack points Republicans levied against Democrats during the Biden administration, tying them to Democrats’ ambitious climate agenda. The reality was a bit more complex — a mixture of travelers emerging from the pandemic, reluctance from oil majors to invest in new drilling and, of course, the war in Ukraine that led to sanctions on Russian energy — but Democrats still suffered at the polls.

Republicans could, too. The war in Iran remains controversial, with 40 percent of respondents to a Washington Post poll last week <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/car-ln-tr/46fb232/69bd1f2d65c20309a123a238/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/22/57/69bd1f2d65c20309a123a238> opposing the U.S. military campaign. The conflict has a more direct, and swift, impact on fuel prices than the Biden administration’s climate agenda.

The Trump administration seems aware of this liability. President Donald Trump has been pleading with U.S. allies to help restore traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. If the strait remains closed, ships could back up, storage capacity in oil-producing Gulf states could max out and oil production itself could cease. It would take weeks or months in that case to get oil pumping out of the ground again once traffic is restored through the strait.

The Trump administration is considering an array of actions to cool the price of oil, including lifting sanctions on Iranian oil that’s already out for export. (Mind you, doing so would put money back in the coffers of the Iranian regime that is conducting retaliatory strikes on U.S. and allied targets). The administration already lifted sanctions on some Russian oil last week in a bid to add more supply to the global market. Rachel Chason, Evan Halper, Victoria Craw, Siham Shamalakh and Tara Copp have more <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/car-ln-tr/470ef32/69bd1f2d65c20309a123a238/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/23/57/69bd1f2d65c20309a123a238>.

Trump also tried to rein in Israel’s attacks on the South Pars gas field — a major natural gas field split between Qatari and Iranian control — as energy markets face increasing instability.

“I told him don’t do that,” Trump told reporters yesterday, apparently referring to Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu.

On the TSA front, Congress still appears a long way from reopening the Department of Homeland Security, which has been shut down since mid-February. Democrats refuse to fund the department unless Congress passes measures to temper federal immigration enforcement efforts. Republicans are advocating a DHS funding bill that the House passed earlier this month. The White House and Senate Democrats have traded offers, but Democrats are insistent on their demands.

Democrats have pitched funding TSA and other non-immigration related aspects of the department to allow workers to collect their paychecks without giving up their fight on immigration enforcement. Republicans have refused.

“The only question Republicans must answer today is this: Has enough been enough?” Senate Minority Leader Charles E. Schumer (D-New York) said on the Senate floor yesterday. "Will they finally fund TSA or keep holding it and our travelers hostage?”

Congress is set to head into its own spring break the week after next, when members will fly home and spend time in their districts. Everett B. Kelley, president of the AFGE union, wrote to lawmakers yesterday urging them to cancel their recess until Congress can find a funding measure to pay TSA workers. Kelley wrote that DHS staffers are turning to selling plasma and visiting food banks to make ends meet and that an extended shutdown would jeopardize national security.

Senate Majority Leader John Thune (R-South Dakota) said he would keep the chamber in town if the shutdown continues beyond next week.

“I can’t see us taking a break if the government’s still shut down,” he said.
