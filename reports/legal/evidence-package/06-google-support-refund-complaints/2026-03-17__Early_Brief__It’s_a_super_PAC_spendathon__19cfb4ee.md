(Scott Olson/Getty Images) Tired: Super Tuesday. Wired: Super PAC Tuesday. It’s primary day in Illinois, and this state’s races are a test case in the power of big money. Super PACs have spent more than $31 million in support of their preferred candidates in this year’s House Democratic primaries alone, advancing interests ranging from Israel to crypto. And because Illinois is one of the most gerrymandered states in the Union, today’s primaries will likely decide the state’s representation in Congress for years to come. In case you need it, here’s a refresher on what makes a super PAC so super. Candidates for federal elected office have limits on how much individual donors can give to their campaigns (the exact amount varies by office). But ever since the 2010 Citizens United case in the Supreme Court, corporations, interest groups and anyone with access to a lot of capital can spend unlimited amounts on “independent expenditures," or campaigning for candidates or causes. The key is super PACs do not coordinate with the official campaigns. (Super PACs are not dark money groups. Super PACs have to eventually disclose their donors. Dark money groups can keep donors anonymous but cannot be engaged exclusively in campaigning.) Advertisement If Illinois shows us anything, it’s that the ban on coordination works in both directions. Interest groups can spend as much as they want on their preferred candidates and candidates can shrug and assert they never courted that group’s money. That can be helpful with polarizing causes among the Democratic base — like Israel or crypto — spending big. Rejecting corporate PAC money is also just plain good politics when public perception is largely against interest group spending in elections. In the race to succeed Senate Minority Leader Dick Durbin (the no. 2 Democrat, retiring after this year having spent nearly three decades in the Senate), Rep. Raja Krishnamoorthi is running with the support of pro-crypto super PAC Fairshake, which has spent about $10 million supporting his bid. Fairshake’s investment in the race has led to concerns that an industry that has also supported Republicans in the past could have undue influence on the state’s next senator, who could have the seat for decades. “I’m deeply concerned that Senator Durbin could be replaced by someone who is deeply compromised,” Sen. Tammy Duckworth (D-Illinois), who is supporting a bid by Lt. Gov. Juliana Stratton, told our colleagues Theodoric Meyer and Mariana Alfaro. Stratton has publicly denounced her primary opponents, casting herself as the only candidate to reject corporate PAC contributions. Krishnamoorthi shot back that Stratton could just as easily be beholden to Gov. JB Pritzker, who is spending millions to support a pro-Stratton super PAC. Rep. Robin Kelly is also running the in the primary and has criticized both for benefitting from patrons, whether it be the crypto industry or Pritzker. “It’s the teapot calling the kettle black as far as what they’re accusing each other of,” Kelly, who is trailing behind Stratton and Krishnamoorthi in public polling, told our colleagues. Advertisement In several House races, super PACs that appear to be backed by pro-Israel or pro-AI interests are also pouring money behind their preferred candidates. Pro-AI interests have spent $2.2 million in the state’s U.S. House primaries, including to support former congresswoman Melissa Bean, who is running to replace Krishnamoorthi in the House. And in three House races, pro-Israel donors from around the country are directing millions of dollars toward candidates that appear to be backed by the American Israel Public Affairs Committee: Bean, Cook County Commissioner Donna Miller and state Sen. Laura Fine. Meanwhile groups that also appear to be backed by the powerful pro-Israel group are spending millions on ads to support the same candidates. On both of those fronts, the PACs’ ads often have nothing to do with AI or Israel. One ad by pro-AI group Think Big PAC — the Democratic affiliate of Leading the Future PAC — in support of Bean focuses on her work on affordable health care and the Consumer Finance Protection Bureau. There is no mention of artificial intelligence in the ad. Two of the groups that appear to be backed by AIPAC have no public ties to the pro-Israel lobby and are running ads focusing on issues ranging from immigration enforcement — one of the biggest motivating issues in this primary — to health care. But that hasn’t stopped candidates from drawing the link and accusing their opponents from benefitting from a group whose support among the Democratic base has soured. Miller, Fine, Bean and Melissa Conyears Ervin all assert they are running on issues that are important to their districts and cannot coordinate with any outside interest groups that happens to be spending on their races. “Anyone who donates to Laura Fine is supporting a woman who will fight for Medicare for All and to abolish ICE,” a spokesperson for Fine wrote to us when we covered the pro-Israel lobby’s involvement in the primary. “Laura has called on every Super PAC in this race to disclose their donors. In Congress, she’ll vote for reforms like those included in the For The People Act to get dark money out of politics." Other candidates on those races have also received support from super PACs. Evanston Mayor Daniel Biss, who is running against Fine and has accused her of benefitting from AIPAC, has the support of J Street Action Fund, another super PAC backing pro-Israel candidates who are more willing to criticize the current Israeli government. Independent expenditures for his candidacy total more than $1 million. Jesse Jackson Jr., who is running against Miller, is also getting a boost of more than $1 million from Think Big PAC. Illinois is hardly the only example of this kind of spending, and many of the players in these races are standard fixtures in campaign spending. The Congressional Progressive Caucus PAC and Medicare for All PAC, for example, are both backing Biss and hardly surprising actors here. But the scale of the outside spending is something to behold for a primary, and the tactics used to obfuscate who’s behind the ads are novel. Part of the attention on Illinois is that it has some of the earliest primaries, and the groups’ successes or losses today could be telling about their efforts in races across the country, from New York to North Carolina . “I’m worried that we have a handful of wealthy folks who think that they’re the ones who are going to call all the shots,” Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-Massachusetts) said at a Stratton rally in Chicago on Friday. “That they will simply drop their money in one election after another and then they’ll own it. ... It’s all in secret. It treats you, it treats voters just like chumps.” For more, read our coverage of the influence of crypto , artificial intelligence and pro-Israel lobbies in today’s elections. Weeks after starting a conflict with Iran, the president appears to be on his heels over the way that Iran has effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, the all-important shipping lane that is critical to worldwide oil prices. Trump took to social media over the weekend to demand “many countries” send ships to the region to help keep the strait “open and safe.” He specifically wrote that he hoped “China, France, Japan, South Korea, the UK, and others” would send ships so that the critical shipping lane “will no longer be a threat by a Nation that has been totally decapitated.” He furthered this request while speaking with reporters on Air Force One. “I’m demanding that these countries come in and protect their own territory because it is their territory,” he said. “It’s the place from which they get their energy.” “Whether we get support or not, I can say this, and I said it to them: We will remember,” Trump said. And Trump even suggested, in an interview with the Financial Times, that he could delay his upcoming trip to China due to their lack of help in the Strait of Hormuz. Trump confirmed yesterday that he requested a delay by “a month or so” due to the war. It’s a remarkable request – the United States and Israel began this conflict, not the nations that Trump is now demanding help in the region – and it highlights the pressure the president is feeling over the way Iran’s effective closure of the strait has led oil prices to spike, markets to drop and roiled part of his base . The response has been equally telling. Australian officials said they will not send ships . Japan’s Defense Minister said they had no plans to follow Trump’s directive . And a spokesperson for German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said no to Trump’s demand , telling reporters that “this war has nothing to do with NATO. It's not NATO’s war.” Trump claimed on Monday that countries have already agreed to help in the strait, but declined to announce them. “I have to tell you, we have some that are really enthusiastic,” Trump said. “They’re coming already. Wired: Super PAC Tuesday.

It’s primary day in Illinois, and this state’s races are a test case in the power of big money.

Super PACs have spent more than $31 million in support of their preferred candidates in this year’s House Democratic primaries alone, advancing interests ranging from Israel to crypto. And because Illinois is one of the most gerrymandered states in the Union, today’s primaries will likely decide the state’s representation in Congress for years to come.

In case you need it, here’s a refresher on what makes a super PAC so super. Candidates for federal elected office have limits on how much individual donors can give to their campaigns (the exact amount varies by office). But ever since the 2010 Citizens United case in the Supreme Court, corporations, interest groups and anyone with access to a lot of capital can spend unlimited amounts on “independent expenditures," or campaigning for candidates or causes. The key is super PACs do not coordinate with the official campaigns.

(Super PACs are not dark money groups. Super PACs have to eventually disclose their donors. Dark money groups can keep donors anonymous but cannot be engaged exclusively in campaigning.)

If Illinois shows us anything, it’s that the ban on coordination works in both directions. Interest groups can spend as much as they want on their preferred candidates and candidates can shrug and assert they never courted that group’s money. That can be helpful with polarizing causes among the Democratic base — like Israel or crypto — spending big. Rejecting corporate PAC money is also just plain good politics when public perception is largely against interest group spending in elections.

In the race to succeed Senate Minority Leader Dick Durbin (the no. 2 Democrat, retiring after this year having spent nearly three decades in the Senate), Rep. Raja Krishnamoorthi is running with the support of pro-crypto super PAC Fairshake, which has spent about $10 million supporting his bid. Fairshake’s investment in the race has led to concerns that an industry that has also supported Republicans in the past could have undue influence on the state’s next senator, who could have the seat for decades.

“I’m deeply concerned that Senator Durbin could be replaced by someone who is deeply compromised,” Sen. Tammy Duckworth (D-Illinois), who is supporting a bid by Lt. Gov. Juliana Stratton, told our colleagues Theodoric Meyer and Mariana Alfaro. Stratton has publicly denounced her primary opponents, casting herself as the only candidate to reject corporate PAC contributions.

Krishnamoorthi shot back that Stratton could just as easily be beholden to Gov. JB Pritzker, who is spending millions to support a pro-Stratton super PAC. Rep. Robin Kelly is also running the in the primary and has criticized both for benefitting from patrons, whether it be the crypto industry or Pritzker.

“It’s the teapot calling the kettle black as far as what they’re accusing each other of,” Kelly, who is trailing behind Stratton and Krishnamoorthi in public polling, told our colleagues.

On both of those fronts, the PACs’ ads often have nothing to do with AI or Israel. In Congress, she’ll vote for reforms like those included in the For The People Act to get dark money out of politics."

Independent expenditures for his candidacy total more than $1 million. Jesse Jackson Jr., who is running against Miller, is also getting a boost of more than $1 million from Think Big PAC.

Illinois is hardly the only example of this kind of spending, and many of the players in these races are standard fixtures in campaign spending. The Congressional Progressive Caucus PAC and Medicare for All PAC, for example, are both backing Biss and hardly surprising actors here. But the scale of the outside spending is something to behold for a primary, and the tactics used to obfuscate who’s behind the ads are novel.
