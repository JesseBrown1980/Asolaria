In today’s edition … Cuba hawks are not breaking with Trump ... The Supreme Court takes up a crucial citizenship case … but first … The big news President Donald Trump has a big day in store — one that could say a great deal about this moment in his presidency. Trump told reporters yesterday that he plans to go to today’s Supreme Court oral arguments on the legality of the president’s year-old executive order on ending birthright citizenship in the United States. “I’m going,” he said. “Because I have listened to this argument for so long.” It’s unclear whether the president will follow through on this comment — he said he would attend oral arguments when his tariff regime reached the court in the fall and didn’t. Today, attending is on his official public schedule. If he does, a president attending Supreme Court arguments is likely without modern precedent. (Scroll down for much more on what is at stake at the Supreme Court) Last night, White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt announced that Trump would deliver a prime-time address to “provide an important update on Iran.” It’s unclear what the president will announce, but the speech comes at a perilous time in the conflict, with gas prices spiking, the stock market jittery and overall support of the war slipping. An oil shipment isn’t a white flag for Cuba hawks An oil tanker sails on Matanzas Bay, Cuba. (Yamil Lage/AFP/Getty Images) A Russian oil tanker arrived in Cuba yesterday, carrying more than 700,000 barrels of oil. The week before, roughly 1 million barrels of oil arrived in the country from Venezuela. Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum isn’t ruling out resuming oil shipments to the island country, which is buckling under a U.S.-imposed energy blockade. The arrival of new energy appears to be a drastic reversal of Trump’s earlier strategy toward the country: Starve the country of oil to topple the regime. After his operation this year to oust the leader of Cuba’s biggest oil supplier, Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro, Trump posted on Truth Social that “THERE WILL BE NO MORE OIL OR MONEY GOING TO CUBA — ZERO!” Advertisement But Trump and his hawkish allies don’t view the shipments as lifting the pressure off of Cuba. “What he’s doing is just doing a small, little humanitarian gesture,” Rep. Maria Elvira Salazar (R-Florida), a longtime critic of Cuba’s communist government, told us. “The [Cuban] regime is not going to be able to survive by receiving one tanker every three months. ... We know that the pressure is on max.” The Trump administration issued a blockade of energy imports into Cuba in January, shortly after the operation to oust Maduro, with Trump openly saying he wanted the removal of Cuban President Miguel Díaz-Canel from office. The blockade has been a devastating blow to the country’s already fractured economy. Cuba produces roughly 40 percent of its fuel needs domestically. The shortage has led to blackouts throughout the country, jeopardizing Cuba’s food security and health care system. The pressure campaign was welcome for many members of the Cuban diaspora community in the U.S., especially as Trump proved increasingly willing to engage in military interventions overseas. “This is the first administration with a Republican or Democrat that has had the fortitude, the desire, the political courage to go in,” Salazar said. “That’s exactly what the Iranians and the Venezuelans and the Cubans want. No one else has the tomahawks or the missiles or the military might as the United States. ... The president has said that Cuba is next, and we’re going to believe him.” So far, the administration’s approach to Cuba appears to be focused on economically pressuring Havana rather than military intervention. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who fiercely advocated for regime change in Cuba when he was a senator from Florida, told Al Jazeera this week that “if Cuba really wants to emerge from the hole they’ve put themselves in,” then “they really need to undertake very serious reforms of both their government and of their economy. And if they’re willing to do so, we’re going to help them. We’ve done nothing punitive against the Cuban regime.” The Cuban government denounced the blockade as intentionally starving the country. Cuba has been under a formal trade embargo by the U.S. since 1962, but it didn’t include a comprehensive blockade of foreign oil imports. Cuba continued to trade with other countries, including U.S. allies such as the European Union, under the post-1962 embargo. “A sign of the brutal siege that Cubans endure with heroism and stoicism. A display of the criminal cruelty of imperialism against a nation that refuses to be dominated,” Carlos de Cossio, Cuba’s deputy foreign minister, wrote on X of the oil blockade. Our colleagues Anthony Faiola and Samantha Schmidt reported last week that the Cuban government has been in talks with the Trump administration through the Catholic Church to reach a resolution (the Vatican was a key conduit to the detente between Havana and the Obama administration in 2015). The assurances from Trump and his allies that the pressure remains high on Havana haven’t stopped Democrats from capitalizing on the overture to Russian oil as another example of Trump backing off on his threats to U.S. adversaries. Florida Democratic Senate candidate Alexander Vindman (Yes, that Alexander Vindman, who was the whistleblower on Trump’s pressure campaign on Ukraine that led to his first impeachment.) attacked one of his main opponents for not calling out the Russian ship bringing oil relief to the island, saying the shipment was capitulating to Havana. “The Cuban people suffer under the brutal Castro dictatorship and Ashley Moody stays silent while millions of dollars’ worth of Russian oil flows to prop up the regime. This is a lifeline to a system that jails dissidents, crushes protests, and denies basic freedoms,” Vindman said in a statement yesterday, going after the incumbent Republican senator. “At this moment the Cuban regime led by Miguel Díaz-Canel is weaker than it has been in years — why is Ashley Moody refusing to speak out? Why is she allowing a critical opportunity for real pressure and reform to slip away?” Moody’s campaign didn’t respond to a request for comment, but Trump and his allies have brushed off the Russian tanker as relatively insignificant. “If a country wants to send some oil into Cuba right now, I have no problem with it,” Trump said on Sunday. “It’s not going to have an impact. Cuba’s finished.” In 2025, Cuba’s oil imports averaged 1.2 million barrels a month , according to S&P Global, with the top importers being Venezuela, Mexico, Russia and Algeria. It received no shipments in January and February , and Mexico and Algeria have still not resumed shipments. Trump has threatened tariffs on countries that ship oil to Cuba. The administration allowed the sale of Venezuelan oil to Cuba in February for private consumers, but maintained a block on sales to the Cuban government. The Russian tanker has enough oil to power the country’s grid for a few weeks. Leavitt said Monday that the administration was allowing in shipments on a “case-by-case” basis for humanitarian reasons. The U.S. intercepting a Russian shipment of oil, which was escorted by the Russian navy, would have been a considerable military confrontation of a major U.S. adversary — and a much bigger escalation than stopping Mexico from shipping oil to Cuba. Cuban state media openly called the Russian tanker a direct challenge to American ability to enforce the blockade. When Leavitt was asked why Russia was allowed to ship oil and not Mexico, she said there was no change in the overall sanctions policy, and U.S. forces still “reserve the right to seize vessels” but also “reserve the right to waive those seizures.” Our picks Retirees receive six times more in federal dollars than young people Americans have little appetite for sending U.S. troops to Iran, polls show Supreme Court rules against ban on ‘conversion therapy’ for LGBTQ+ minors Judge halts construction on Trump’s ballroom, says Congress must decide Gas hits $4 a gallon for first time since 2022, and costs may keep rising What we’re watching The Supreme Court today will hear oral arguments in a case that will decide the future of birthright citizenship in the U.S. Trump signed an executive order on his first day in office last year that aimed to end birthright citizenship, directing U.S. government agencies to “no longer issue citizenship documentation to babies born in the United States to parents who lack legal status.” The case, which comes to the high court after a federal court blocked Trump’s order, centers on the citizenship clause of the 14th Amendment, which grants citizenship to “All persons born or naturalized in the United States, and subject to the jurisdiction thereof.” This was one of many election-related orders signed by the president in the first weeks of his presidency, as Trump looked to make good on his hardline approach to immigration. The U.S., which grants citizenship based on jus soli, or “right of soil,” is in the minority of nations, with most countries following the principle of jus sanguinis, or “right of blood,” which links citizenship to the citizenship of their parents, not where they are born. Trump’s commentary on this issue has led many to believe he thinks he will lose at the Supreme Court. “Dumb Judges and Justices will not a great Country make,” he wrote earlier this week ahead of arguments. Democrats and immigration proponents have hammered Trump on this issue, and a group of congressional Democrats submitted a brief to the Supreme Court warning that if the Trump administration were to prevail, “millions of Americans will suddenly no longer be citizens” and could lose the right to vote, secure a passport and more. Our colleague Justin Jouvenal also wrote earlier this week about how the federal government’s arguments in the case cite noted white supremacists as the Trump administration looks to fully change who becomes a citizen in the United States. No matter what happens, these arguments and the eventual ruling will be a major moment for the country and the Trump administration, given that the president has so steadfastly campaigned on redefining what it means to be an American.
