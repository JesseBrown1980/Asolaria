Supreme Court skeptical about Trump’s birthright citizenship push … but first … The big news Donald Trump last night used one of the biggest platforms available to a president to tell Americans that the nation’s conflict with Iran is “nearing completion” — a variation of what he has been saying for weeks. Trump’s presidential address was not all that new. Instead, it was largely a retelling of what he had already said about the conflict: The president pledged to “never” allow Iran to have a nuclear weapon. He told Americans that the nation’s military is “going to hit [Iran] extremely hard over the next two to three weeks,” and yet, the war will end “shortly, very shortly.” Trump, however, has been saying for weeks that the war is almost over. On March 9, he said the conflict was “pretty well complete.” He wrote on March 20 that the nation was “getting very close to meeting our objectives.” And earlier this week, he told reporters the conflict would be ending “very soon.” Then there was Trump’s inconsistent messaging on regime change. “Regime change was not our goal. We never said regime change,” Trump said. That, however, is not true. Trump has openly rooted for regime change in Tehran. “It’s time to look for new leadership in Iran,” he told Politico in January. He said in mid-February, before the conflict began, that Iranian regime change “would be the best thing. ” And since the war began, he has encouraged Iranians to overthrow their government and claimed that regime change has happened. What the speech told us is that this is a critical moment for the president as he weighs possible operations on the ground in Iran . The war has grown unpopular, with a CNN poll released this week finding that only 34 percent of Americans now approve at least somewhat of the decision to take military action in Iran, down since the start of the conflict. (Joe Raedle/Getty Images) Democrats running in competitive primaries have found a new, potent attack against their opponents: ties to Palantir, the large data management company that is helping the Trump administration track undocumented immigrants and deport them faster . The attacks, which stem from candidates holding stock in Palantir, receiving donations from company executives or interacting with the company in past jobs, seize on Democratic opposition to the president’s immigration policy. Democrats using these attacks hope voters will question a candidate’s authenticity if they are critical of Trump but tied to the technology firm powering his deportations. In a Democratic primary along Florida’s Atlantic coast, Pia Dandiya’s financial disclosure report revealed that she owns up to $15,000 in Palantir stock. And one of her Democratic opponents, James Martin, is eager to talk about it. Advertisement “If we want to end corruption and restore trust in government, we cannot elect representatives beholden to corporations like Palantir or who personally profit from them,” Martin told us. “Voters should not have to trade one special interest‐backed politician for another with a different party label.” Dandiya’s campaign did not respond to a request for comment. The primary between Dandiya and Martin will decide who takes on Rep. Brian Mast (R) in November. Our former colleague Eva Dou did remarkable reporting on the connection between Palantir and ICE in December , writing that the company’s Immigration OS software “plays a key role in supporting the administration’s mass deportation campaign.” This line of attack has become so pervasive that a group named Purge Palantir has started tracking donations the company’s top staff have made to candidates, finding that Democrats including Rep. Adam Smith (Washington), the ranking member of the House Committee on Armed Services; Rep. Josh Riley (New York), a member of the House Committee on Science, Space, and Technology; and Sen. Martin Heinrich (New Mexico) received some of the largest sums from Palantir employees. The tracker has made an impact: Both Rep. Jason Crow (D-Colorado) and Sen. John Hickenlooper (D-Colorado) collectively donated over $100,000 to immigrant rights groups after the Colorado Sun reported in February that they received considerable sums from the controversial company. These attacks were especially potent in the Illinois Senate Democratic primary, in which Lt. Gov. Juliana Stratton repeatedly attacked Rep. Raja Krishnamoorthi for taking money from Shyam Sankar, the chief technology officer at Palantir. Illinois Future PAC, the primary super PAC supporting Stratton, aired a blistering ad in the contest that accused Krishnamoorthi of “having something to hide,” invoking the donations he has received from Sankar. “He’s been taking donations from an ICE contractor for 10 years,” says a narrator, before noting the uptick in federal immigration enforcement last year in Chicago. “When Chicago was being raided, he was taking money from an ICE contractor. ... When Illinois needed a fighter, Raja Krishnamoorthi sold us out.” Krishnamoorthi, who did not respond to a request for comment, donated the money to unnamed immigration rights groups . But the damage was largely done by millions of dollars worth of ads — the congressman went on to lose the primary to Stratton. Illinois is not unique, however. In New York, Rep. Dan Goldman has attempted to fend off a liberal challenge from Brad Lander, the former New York City comptroller. Goldman has attacked Lander for increasing New York pension fund holdings of Palantir while he oversaw the funds, despite Lander now running as being vehemently opposed to ICE. “These are not normal times,” said Maddy Rosen, spokesperson for the Goldman campaign, accusing Lander of hypocrisy for advocating for immigrants while overseeing an entity that invested in Palantir. “His decision to put profits over people is disqualifying,” Rosen added. Lauren Hitt, a spokesperson for the Lander campaign, said policy around the pension fund barred the candidate from divesting from Palantir, but in Congress, Lander would “work aggressively to stop Palantir and others from aiding Trump’s inhumane immigration policy.” In Texas, former congressman Colin Allred forced Rep. Julie Johnson (D-Texas) into a runoff by attacking the congresswoman for buying and selling Palantir stock. The Texas Tribune broke the story about Johnson’s trades , reporting that she bought the company’s stock twice in early 2025 and sold it later in the year. “It is a question of judgment,” Allred said. “If you are willing, as a sitting member of Congress, to buy and sell stock in this company that is so recognizably part of this mass deportation and surveillance state that this administration is pursuing ... your judgment is questionable.” Johnson hit back, arguing that she divested her entire stock portfolio during her first year in Congress, despite making trades during that time. “My opponent never made any progress on stock ban reform during his three terms in office,” Johnson said. Allred said he supports a stock-trading ban and that he “never traded stocks” during his time in office. And in New Hampshire, Maura Sullivan, a Democrat running in the state’s 1st Congressional District, was criticized for accepting $9,000 in donations from people connected to Palantir. The Union Leader reported on the donations in March , noting that Sullivan, a veteran of the Iraq War, refused to return the donations because they came from personal friends, including the godfather of her youngest child. Lisa Gordon, a spokesperson for Palantir, touted how the company’s partnership with the government leads to “remarkable things that help improve the lives of millions of people.” “Despite persistent misunderstandings about what we do, we will continue to work with Democrats and Republicans alike to help our government work more effectively on behalf of all Americans while upholding our enduring commitment to protecting privacy and civil liberties,” Gordon said. This trend, however, highlights just how toxic Trump’s immigration policy can be to the Democratic base, and how other Democrats see there are votes to be gained in tying an opponent to the power behind that policy. “What most folks are seeing, they recognize as being deeply un-American,” Allred said of the anti-ICE sentiment among Democratic voters. “It is happening on TV, it is happening on our cellphones. The high court heard arguments over an executive order Trump signed early last year that aimed to limit birthright citizenship to babies born of U.S. citizens and foreigners with residency in the country. Notably, the children of undocumented immigrants and short-term visitors would not get citizenship. It’s a major priority for Trump, so much so that he showed up to the court in person . Presidents pretty much never go to Supreme Court arguments. The executive order has been on pause while it faces legal challenges. The White House argument hinges on a line in the 14th Amendment that says children born on American soil and “subject to the jurisdiction” of the U.S. will be granted citizenship. The “subject to the jurisdiction” line is widely understood to exempt the children of diplomats who enjoy diplomatic immunity and the children of invading armies from being granted citizenship. Those groups are not subject to U.S. laws. The White House asserts undocumented immigrants are also not subject to the jurisdiction of the U.S. because they do not have demonstrated allegiance to the country. But immigrants are, in fact, subject to U.S. laws and can be arrested and tried for breaking them. “The examples you give to support that strike me as very quirky … children of ambassadors, children of enemies during a hostile invasion, children on warships. And then you expand it to a whole class of illegal aliens here in the country,” Chief Justice John G. Roberts Jr. said. “I’m not quite sure how you can get to that big group from such tiny and sort of idiosyncratic examples.” Read more from Justin Jouvenal on the arguments.
