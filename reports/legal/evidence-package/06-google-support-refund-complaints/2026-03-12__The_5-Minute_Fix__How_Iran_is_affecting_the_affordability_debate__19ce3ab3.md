Yes, keep this newsletter By Amber Phillips It’s safe to say the last thing Republicans wanted in an election year was for gas prices to jump because of actions President Donald Trump has taken. But that’s where they are as the war with Iran stretches into its second week and rages across the Middle East with no clear end in sight. Yet it’s also unclear just how much this presents an opening for Democrats, who are trying to retake one or even both chambers of Congress in November’s midterm elections on the promise of bringing down costs. Affordability is the No. 1 topic in American politics right now, and the war could have a major impact on it. Here’s what’s going on. The war is unpopular, but … More than half of Americans oppose the U.S. military action against Iran, a Quinnipiac University poll released Monday found. But there’s also evidence that a sizable chunk of Americans are still making up their minds about it. A pair of Washington Post surveys taken when the war was in its second day and second week found noticeable growth in the number of Americans who said the strikes should continue. These aren’t horrible numbers for Trump, given 65 percent in the most recent Post survey also said the Trump administration hasn’t clearly explained why the U.S. launched this to begin with. So there’s a way to take advantage of Republican and Republican-leaning voters’ goodwill, say strategists: The Trump administration can try to lower gas prices in the short term (by releasing reserves of oil, for instance) and find a way to end the conflict in a way that prevents Iran from sabotaging key oil shipping routes. But all of that would probably have to happen in the next few weeks. “What we are seeing on the ground with voters is they are willing to give Trump a little more time — they understand even though they’re not thrilled that if he can accomplish this, it would be the first time in generations there’s been peace in the Middle East,” said Sarah Chamberlain, president of the Republican Main Street Partnership, which supports Republican lawmakers in competitive congressional districts. “But the summer driving season hasn’t started yet. My prediction is voters will wait a month to six weeks.” Success in Iran — whatever that looks like — is a long shot in that time frame, argue some military experts. “We are flying blind, captured by magical thinking,” argued a team of military experts with the left-leaning Brookings Institution last week. “ … It’s highly uncertain that the U.S.-Israeli operation will successfully produce a real transition to a different regime.” Advertisement Another goal the Trump administration has put forth is simply resecuring the Strait of Hormuz to ensure that whenever the conflict does end, Iran can’t trap or attack oil tankers there. Some experts also see that as a heavy lift, given economic warfare is a main lever a defiant Iran can use. “We are currently experiencing what is the largest oil supply disruption in history,” warned Gregory Brew, who analyzes Iran and energy for the Eurasia Group, in a panel hosted this week by the Cato Institute. “Energy prices will remain high.” The longer this war goes on, it increases the risk of rising grocery prices, the costs of goods and even flights. Democrats see an easy opening, but ... Democrats argue that Trump is neither good at making policy nor selling what he’s done. On social media this week, he argued that oil prices going up were actually good for the economy: “The United States is the largest Oil Producer in the World, by far, so when oil prices go up, we make a lot of money,” he posted. Advertisement Trump also said recently that affordability is a “fake word” and promised to continue his unpopular tariffs even after the Supreme Court knocked many of them down. He’s unveiled a website aimed at helping people find drug discounts but has let popular subsidies to help people afford health care on Affordable Care Act exchanges expire. “It’s pretty straightforward,” said Democratic strategist Jesse Ferguson when asked how Democrats take advantage of this kind of messaging. “From health care premiums to tariffs and now gas prices, he’s making everything harder and more expensive. The checkout line, the kitchen table or the gas pump — they’re all basically making our case.” Still, it doesn’t seem like that message has landed with voters yet. With all of Trump’s problems, a new NBC News poll finds that a majority of registered voters dislike both parties but slightly more voters view the Republican Party favorably (37 percent) than the Democratic Party (30 percent). It’s safe to say the last thing Republicans wanted in an election year was for gas prices to jump  <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/46fc77e/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/13/50/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a>because of actions President Donald Trump has taken. But that’s where they are as the war with Iran stretches into its second week and rages across the Middle East with no clear end in sight.

Yet it’s also unclear just how much this presents an opening for Democrats, who are trying to retake one or even both chambers of Congress in November’s midterm elections on the promise of bringing down costs.

Affordability is the No. 1 topic in American politics right now, and the war could have a major impact on it. Here’s what’s going on.
The war is unpopular, but …
More than half of Americans oppose the U.S. military action against Iran, a Quinnipiac University poll <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/46fc77f/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/14/50/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a> released Monday found. But there’s also evidence that a sizable chunk of Americans are still making up their minds about it. A pair of Washington Post surveys <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/46fc780/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/15/50/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a> taken when the war was in its second day and second week found noticeable growth in the number of Americans who said the strikes should continue.

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=987489&li=thefix&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a&stpe=default>

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=987489&li=thefix&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a&stpe=default>

These aren’t horrible numbers for Trump, given 65 percent in the most recent Post survey also said the Trump administration hasn’t clearly explained why the U.S. launched this to begin with.

So there’s a way to take advantage of Republican and Republican-leaning voters’ goodwill, say strategists: The Trump administration can try to lower gas prices in the short term (by releasing reserves of oil, for instance) and find a way to end the conflict in a way that prevents Iran from sabotaging key oil shipping routes.

But all of that would probably have to happen in the next few weeks.

“What we are seeing on the ground with voters is they are willing to give Trump a little more time — they understand even though they’re not thrilled that if he can accomplish this, it would be the first time in generations there’s been peace in the Middle East,” said Sarah Chamberlain, president of the Republican Main Street Partnership, which supports Republican lawmakers in competitive congressional districts. “But the summer driving season hasn’t started yet. My prediction is voters will wait a month to six weeks.”

Success in Iran — whatever that looks like — is a long shot in that time frame, argue some military experts. “We are flying blind, captured by magical thinking,” argued <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/46fc781/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/18/50/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a> a team of military experts with the left-leaning Brookings Institution last week. “ … It’s highly uncertain that the U.S.-Israeli operation will successfully produce a real transition to a different regime.”

Another goal the Trump administration has put forth is simply resecuring the Strait of Hormuz to ensure that whenever the conflict does end, Iran can’t trap or attack oil tankers there. Some experts also see that as a heavy lift, given economic warfare is a main lever a defiant Iran can use.

“We are currently experiencing what is the largest oil supply disruption in history,” warned Gregory Brew, who analyzes Iran and energy for the Eurasia Group, in a panel hosted this week by the Cato Institute. “Energy prices will remain high.”

The longer this war goes on, it increases the risk of <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/46fc77e/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/20/50/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a> rising grocery prices, the costs of goods and even flights.
Democrats see an easy opening, but ...
Democrats argue that Trump is neither good at making policy nor selling what he’s done. On social media this week, he argued that oil prices going up were actually good for the economy: “The United States is the largest Oil Producer in the World, by far, so when oil prices go up, we make a lot of money,” he posted.

Trump also said recently that affordability is a “fake word” and promised to continue his unpopular tariffs even after the Supreme Court knocked many of them down. He’s unveiled a website <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/46adcb0/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/22/50/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a> aimed at helping people find drug discounts but has let popular subsidies to help people afford health care on Affordable Care Act exchanges expire.

“It’s pretty straightforward,” said Democratic strategist Jesse Ferguson when asked how Democrats take advantage of this kind of messaging. “From health care premiums to tariffs and now gas prices, he’s making everything harder and more expensive. The checkout line, the kitchen table or the gas pump — they’re all basically making our case.”

Still, it doesn’t seem like that message has landed with voters yet. With all of Trump’s problems, a new NBC News poll <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/46fc782/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/23/50/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a> finds that a majority of registered voters dislike both parties but slightly more voters view the Republican Party favorably (37 percent) than the Democratic Party (30 percent).

Democrats staked out a strong position on health care prices during a government shutdown this fall but haven’t yet united on a message about how they’ll work to lower prices, say some strategists.

“There’s a bunch of places where Democrats are still not trusted as much as Republicans,” MS NOW anchor Chris Hayes told <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/46fc783/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/24/50/69b31d2c8ba9e962d064f92a> the New York Times this week, “things like immigration, crime, the economy.”
