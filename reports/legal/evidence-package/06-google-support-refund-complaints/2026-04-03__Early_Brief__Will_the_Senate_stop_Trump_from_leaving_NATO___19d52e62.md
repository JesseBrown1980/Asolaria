Tim Kaine (D-Virginia) worked on legislation in 2023 with then-Sen. Marco Rubio (R-Florida) that would require a supermajority of the Senate to consent for a president to leave NATO. (Sarah L. Voisin/The Washington Post) Before he was secretary of state, Marco Rubio was a hawkish Republican senator who ardently supported NATO. He worked with Sen. Tim Kaine (D-Virginia) in 2023 on legislation that would require a supermajority of the Senate to consent for a president to leave the alliance. The legislation passed with an overwhelming bipartisan majority and may soon be put to the test as Trump continues to deride the alliance, which turns 77 tomorrow. But the Senate’s work defending NATO doesn’t end there, Kaine told us. The chamber, which includes Republicans fiercely supportive of NATO, has been busy lately assuring NATO allies that they have no intention of letting the country exit any time soon. Advertisement "It was a message to our NATO allies that, hey, Congress is important, and Congress, in a bipartisan way, really believes in the power of this alliance,” Kaine, a member of the Senate Armed Services and Foreign Relations committees, said of the measure. “Those of us who do armed services and foreign relations work are spending an awful lot of time now trying to reassure allies that they still have friends on both sides of the aisle in Congress.” Trump, never a big fan of NATO, went on a rampage this week criticizing NATO allies for refusing to more robustly support the U.S. offensive in Iran. He has demanded NATO allies do more to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, a key shipping lane, and told the Telegraph that he was seriously considering dropping out of the alliance. “I was never swayed by NATO. I always knew they were a paper tiger,” Trump told the British newspaper. NATO leaders have increasingly expressed frustration with Trump’s stance. French President Emmanuel Macron told reporters yesterday that Trump and Israel can “deplore” all they like, but they started the war without consulting NATO first. Trump made similar threats to leave the alliance during his first term, and Kaine worked with Sen. John McCain (R-Arizona) at the time to codify a requirement for congressional consent before the president could leave the treaty. The legislation eventually passed in 2023 as part of an annual defense spending bill, with Rubio taking over as the lead Republican after McCain’s death. All Democrats and 18 Republicans voted for the provision. Both Rubio and Kaine heralded the legislation at the time as a necessary protection for one of the most important global security alliances. Kaine recalled to us getting Rubio on board at the time was “extremely easy. It was not a heavy lift to convince Marco that this was a good idea.” Rubio now joins Trump in questioning the value of the alliance. He told Sean Hannity of Fox News that the U.S. will “have to reexamine whether or not this alliance that has served this country well” and has not become a “one-way street where America is simply in a position to defend Europe.” But Kaine, who is not surprised by Rubio’s change of tune now that he’s a member of Trump’s cabinet, is confident that their legislation will prevent the president from unilaterally exiting the treaty. The law requires the president to notify Congress in advance of any intention to leave NATO and two-thirds of the Senate or an act of Congress to formalize the exit. That is a political impossibility, with Republicans controlling only 53 seats and many of them ardent Atlanticists. Former Senate majority leader Mitch McConnell (R-Kentucky) joined Sen. Chris Coons (D-Delaware) in issuing a statement this week calling NATO “the most successful military alliance in history,” noting its role in defending the U.S. after 9/11. NATO’s assistance in the War on Terror was the only time the alliance’s Article V obligations — which state an attack on any member is an attack on all members — was invoked. “This law is absolutely rock solid,” Kaine said. “If the president says we’re withdrawing from NATO, to me, it would have the same effect as if the president said the sun rises in the west. It would be words, but it would be completely inconsequential and irrelevant words.” Not everyone in the Senate shares that view. Sen. Rand Paul (R-Kentucky) supported Trump leaving the alliance and challenged the constitutionality of requiring Senate approval to exit treaties. The Constitution requires the Senate’s consent to ratify treaties but is silent on quitting them. “I support the President’s constitutional authority under Article II to withdraw from any treaty — including NATO — without needing Senate approval. Congress tried to block that with the 2023 Kaine-Rubio provision in the NDAA, but the Constitution grants the executive power to the president,” Paul posted on X. Under the North Atlantic Treaty, which created the alliance, member states are free to leave after notifying the U.S. government a year in advance. The U.S. is then responsible for distributing the country’s request to exit to other members. What happens if the U.S. is the country leaving? The treaty doesn’t say. In practice, a U.S. departure would likely spell the end of the alliance, said Ian Brzezinski, deputy assistant secretary of defense for Europe and NATO Policy under President George W. Bush. “The thing about NATO is it was U.S.-inspired, U.S.-led because, in part, the predominance of the United States. And for that reason, it has worked, and it has worked effectively,” Brzezinski said. “If the United States were to leave, it would not just be a body blow to European security from an outside aggressor. It’ll be a body blow, I think, to European unity.” Even with congressional guardrails, NATO allies still worry the president could fall back on doing the absolute minimum even if he stays in NATO on paper, said Kaine, who was a couple of minutes late to our interview because he was wrapping up a call with a NATO ambassador. The Senate included a provision in a defense-spending bill last year that would require congressional notification 60 days before a major drawdown of U.S. forces overseas. Still, Brzezinski noted, “operationally, the president, of course, can de facto dilute the effectiveness of our commitment to Article V through his powers as commander in chief and his right to deploy forces where he sees fit.” “You do have to confront the reality that the president saying this — even though he has no authority to do it and can’t do it — it has a destabilizing effect on the alliance, because it makes allies really, really nervous,” Kaine said.
