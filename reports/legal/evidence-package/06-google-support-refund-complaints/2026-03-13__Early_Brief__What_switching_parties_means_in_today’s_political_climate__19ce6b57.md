He’s running for governor as a Democrat. (Chris O'Meara/AP) Can you trust someone who changes their mind? In politics, it can be a hard pitch to sell. Voters value consistency, with changed stances often viewed with suspicion — or even seen as betrayal. Past party switchers have a record of ruin at the ballot box, with critics calling them opportunistic or lacking in principles. But some candidates are making their changed political identities a pillar of their campaign message. “I don’t think the problem in politics today are people who change. It’s people who won’t change,” said former Republican congressman David Jolly, who is running as a Democrat to be governor of Florida. “Because if you’ve decided to stick with this president through the last 10 years, yeah, I have great reservations about that.” Jolly joined the Democratic Party in 2025, eight years after leaving Congress, and has made the transition a central tenet of his campaign. He feels his past as a moderate Republican can help connect with disaffected Floridians who previously voted for President Donald Trump but later came to regret their decision. “Imagine if Hillary Clinton had remained a Republican. She was the [Young Republicans] president at Wellesley. Imagine if Elizabeth Warren had remained a conservative Republican attorney in her 40s, which she was,” Jolly said. “I’ve been out of politics long enough to tell the truth. I don’t run from it. I have changed.” Those are hardly the only examples. Several other candidates have had success after changing parties in the past. Sen. John Neely Kennedy (R-Louisiana) switched from being a longtime, John F. Kerry-supporting Democrat to a Republican while Louisiana state treasurer in 2007. He has since become one of Trump’s most fervent supporters in the Senate and has yet to face a competitive reelection challenge. (He won in 2022 with 61.6 percent of the vote.) Trump has also appointed Democrats to his Cabinet, including Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard (a formerly anti-interventionist Democratic congresswoman) and Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. Rep. Jeff Van Drew (R-New Jersey) switched parties in 2020 after being first elected to Congress as a Democrat in 2018. He went on to lead Trump’s 2024 campaign efforts in New Jersey and is a vocal Trump supporter in the House. He has easily won reelection each cycle since his party switch. But switching parties can also open candidates to criticisms of opportunism. Sen. Arlen Specter (Pennsylvania) flip-flopped twice in his career, from Democrat to Republican in 1965, and Republican to Democrat in 2009. His second switch attracted accusations that he was solely switching parties to avoid a right-wing primary challenge in the 2010 Senate race. Commentators lambasted him as “Benedict Arlen,” and his new adoptive party refused to let him keep his Senate seniority if he won reelection. He lost that year’s Democratic senate primary, and Patrick J. Toomey (R) won the seat in the general election. Advertisement Numerous party switchers — and party abandoners who left to run as independents — assert they haven’t changed. Their party has. Former Republican Florida governor Charlie Crist switched parties to be a Democrat in 2012 after he left office, asserting the Republican Party had “left” him and took a hard tack to the right. He later served as a Democrat in Congress from 2017 to 2022. West Virginia’s former senator Joe Manchin III made the same assertion when he left the Democrats to become an independent in 2024. Formerly Democratic Webb County Judge Tano Tijerina, who is running as a Republican in Texas’s 28th Congressional District, made the same argument in justifying his party switch. The lifelong Democrat changed parties in 2024, asserting he had always been an antiabortion, pro-border security conservative but that the national Democratic Party had lost touch with the values of South Texans. “What the Democrats in Joe Biden’s time were trying to do is shove everything down, all the social issues, when, in all reality, we had some real issues that were going on, with economics, border … infrastructure needs,” Tijerina told us. “Yet all they were talking about is more social, wokeness, wokeness, wokeness. And I think that’s why they lost everybody.” Tijerina’s move away from the party reflects broader trends in South Texas, where Democrats long held a monopoly on power despite residents tending to be more religious and socially conservative. Republicans didn’t start winning electorally in the area until the 2022 special election in the Rio Grande Valley that saw Mayra Flores win a seat in the House and that year’s general election with Rep. Monica De La Cruz winning another seat in the valley. Before De La Cruz and Flores, running as a Democrats was essentially the only way to be politically relevant in the region, regardless of personal beliefs. Republicans view Tijerina’s evolution from conservative Democrat to Republican as a relatable story to voters in his challenge against 11-term Democratic Rep. Henry Cuellar. Cuellar is a socially conservative Democrat and the last antiabortion member of his party in the House. He won reelection in 2024 despite Trump winning his district by more than seven percentage points. Under the latest district lines passed by the state legislature last year, the district would have voted for Trump in 2024 by more than 10 percentage points. Tijerina said his party change has also led to accusations of political opportunism and that he was simply running as a Republican to ride the new red surge in the region. Is it too late to join the party?

Rep. David Jolly (R-Florida) in 2015. He’s running for governor as a Democrat. (Chris O'Meara/AP)

Can you trust someone who changes their mind?

In politics, it can be a hard pitch to sell. Voters value consistency, with changed stances often viewed with suspicion — or even seen as betrayal. Past party switchers have a record of ruin at the ballot box, with critics calling them opportunistic or lacking in principles.

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=906529&li=powerup&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69b3e4425ad9545be33ec589&stpe=default>

But some candidates are making their changed political identities a pillar of their campaign message.

“I don’t think the problem in politics today are people who change. Jolly joined the Democratic Party in 2025, eight years after leaving Congress, and has made the transition a central tenet of his campaign. “Imagine if Hillary Clinton had remained a Republican. She was the [Young Republicans] president at Wellesley. Imagine if Elizabeth Warren had remained a conservative Republican attorney in her 40s, which she was,” Jolly said. “I’ve been out of politics long enough to tell the truth. I don’t run from it. I have changed.”
