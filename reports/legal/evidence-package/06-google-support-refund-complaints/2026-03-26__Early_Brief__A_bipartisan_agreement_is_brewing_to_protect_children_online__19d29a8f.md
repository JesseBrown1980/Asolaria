(Demetrius Freeman/The Washington Post) Two significant legal decisions this week concern the impact of social media on young people — and may mean the era of tech companies escaping punishment for their practices is coming to an end. First, as our colleague Ian Duncan reported on Tuesday, “A jury in New Mexico sided with the state attorney general’s office in a landmark case accusing social media giant Meta of allowing its platforms to become venues for child predators to solicit young users. The jury ordered Meta to pay $375 million for violations of the state’s consumer protection laws in its verdict.” Meta plans to appeal. Second, on Wednesday, a jury in Los Angeles “found Meta, the operator of Facebook and Instagram, and video platform YouTube negligent and awarded $6 million in damages to a young woman who alleged she had become hooked on the companies’ services as a child.” “The twin verdicts,” Ian wrote, “are signs of cracks in legal protections that for decades made tech companies seem almost impervious, as lawyers accuse the platforms of putting addictive or otherwise harmful features into their products.” Taken together, this is a notable reckoning on the impact social media is having on young people. But it also says a great deal about big tech companies in this unique political moment, when there is true bipartisan agreement that Congress needs to act to protect children online by taking action against these massive American companies. “Protecting children and teens online is galvanizing people from every political divide because they see through Big Tech, and it’s a kitchen table issue that does not discriminate. It affects all of us,” said Sacha Haworth, head of The Tech Oversight Project, an advocacy organization focused on holding big technology companies accountable. “This is a majoritarian issue that demands action. Congressional leadership needs to step up and pass the Senate’s Kids Online Safety Act.” Advertisement These verdicts follow years of warnings that social media was addicting young people. In 2023, Surgeon General Vivek Murthy used a report to call youth mental health “the defining public health issue of our time” and warn that social media was the main contributor to the problem. He also wrote about the issue in The Post. Part of this momentum has manifested in the push to pass the Kids Online Safety Act, a bill that would impose a “reasonable care” standard on social media platforms to protect minors from harmful content. Conservative groups such as American Compass, the America First Policy Institute and the Heritage Foundation, among others, are behind the bill . And Rep. Brett Guthrie (R-Kentucky), chairman of the House Energy and Commerce Committee, recently wrote in this paper that those in Congress “have a special obligation to hold tech companies accountable for the products they design.” The list of politicians getting behind this movement — and in often personal ways — is striking. “Let’s just be honest, we may as well put a sign on the floor of the Senate that says, ‘Property of Big Tech,’ because nothing moves across that floor, in my observation, nothing that they don’t want,” Sen. Josh Hawley (R-Missouri) said during a hearing in December that featured the parents of children who died by suicide after online harassment. Hawley went on to warn that unless lawmakers “act and do something, unless we stand up to these corporate interests, more and more children” will be impacted by social media. Mallory McMorrow, the Michigan Senate majority whip and a candidate for Senate in the closely watched state, has been running on a plan to “keep kids safe online,” which includes banning cellphones in classrooms, requiring social media platforms to ensure “privacy by default” for minor accounts and stopping social media providers that use “addictive features like infinite scroll” from allowing children under 14 to sign up. Advertisement “I struggle enough with my phone, and my screen time is embarrassing, and I also remember the before times when we didn’t have phones and social media, and with a 5-year-old now, and when I’m thinking about my daughter, it just terrifies me thinking about her getting older,” McMorrow told us. “As a mom and somebody who is probably the last generation who remembers the before time, it just feels like the right thing to do.” Rahm Emanuel, a possible 2028 candidate and the former mayor of Chicago, backed Australia’s plan to ban children under 16 from social media and urged the United States to do the same . One reason for this groundswell is simply politicians following the will of the people. A Gallup survey in January found that “big technology is viewed as the biggest threat by 32% of U.S. adults, ranking second to big government at 45% and ahead of big business at 20%.” A Pew Research Survey last year found 48 percent of teens believe social media has a “mostly negative effect on people their age, up from 32% in 2022. ” And a Fox News poll in December 2025 found “nearly two-thirds of voters and parents support banning social media for children under 16 and removing cellphones from K-12 classrooms.” “This is the result of what I’m actually hearing on the ground,” said McMorrow. “It’s the culmination of what people are telling me.” The question now is whether anything will happen. Congress has been light on passing legislation over the last year, and even when there is bipartisan agreement on a topic, its path to the president’s desk is often unclear. “This trial was proof that if you put CEOs like Mark Zuckerberg on the stand before a judge and jury of their peers, the tech industry’s wanton disregard for people will be on full display,” said Haworth, the head of the tech accountability group. “We have the documents, we have the evidence, and now is the time for Congress to step up and finally pass the Senate’s Kids Online Safety Act so that we can finally protect kids and save lives.” Our picks House Democrat accused of misspending covid-19 money could be expelled Trump’s move to send ICE to airports fails to break impasse or end long lines Inside the GOP struggle with bigotry among young activists Politicians are trying to make life cheaper. In Florida, Gregory’s victory, along with that of Democrat Brian Nathan, who also narrowly pulled off an upset in a Tampa-area state Senate special election, has given the out-of-power party a needed boost and raised hopes that the 2026 midterms could bring significant Democratic wins nationwide. “This is one in a trend that we have seen across the country,” Rep. Republicans know they are losing.” Republicans largely dismissed Tuesday’s results as illustrative of a broader trend. “I don’t read too much into it,” said Rep. And there is some truth to the statement that Democrats have become the party that does better in low turnout contests, a flip on years-old conventional wisdom. Two significant legal decisions this week concern the impact of social media on young people — and may mean the era of tech companies escaping punishment for their practices is coming to an end.

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=906529&li=powerup&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69c508032e9f3f059b5f21a8&stpe=default>

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=906529&li=powerup&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69c508032e9f3f059b5f21a8&stpe=default>

First, as our colleague Ian Duncan reported <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/car-ln-tr/471f6b7/69c508032e9f3f059b5f21a8/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/20/60/69c508032e9f3f059b5f21a8> on Tuesday, “A jury in New Mexico sided with the state attorney general’s office in a landmark case accusing social media giant Meta of allowing its platforms to become venues for child predators to solicit young users. Second, on Wednesday, a jury in Los Angeles <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/car-ln-tr/471f6b6/69c508032e9f3f059b5f21a8/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/21/60/69c508032e9f3f059b5f21a8> “found Meta, the operator of Facebook and Instagram, and video platform YouTube negligent and awarded $6 million in damages to a young woman who alleged she had become hooked on the companies’ services as a child.”

But it also says a great deal about big tech companies in this unique political moment, when there is true bipartisan agreement that Congress needs to act <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/car-ln-tr/471f6b8/69c508032e9f3f059b5f21a8/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/22/60/69c508032e9f3f059b5f21a8> to protect children online by taking action against these massive American companies.

“Protecting children and teens online is galvanizing people from every political divide because they see through Big Tech, and it’s a kitchen table issue that does not discriminate. It affects all of us,” said Sacha Haworth, head of The Tech Oversight Project, an advocacy organization focused on holding big technology companies accountable. “This is a majoritarian issue that demands action. Congressional leadership needs to step up and pass the Senate’s Kids Online Safety Act.”
