(Octavio Jones/AFP/Getty Images) President Donald Trump, who is pressuring senators to curb the use of mail-in voting, voted by mail in a special election happening today in Palm Beach County, Florida. That is the news we broke last night in a piece on how Trump uses a mail-in ballot while asking lawmakers to limit the practice . The Palm Beach County Supervisor of Elections website indicates that Trump, who is registered to vote at his Mar-a-Lago estate on Palm Beach, “voted by Mail Ballot” in a special election between Democrat Emily Gregory and Republican Jon Maples for a seat in the state legislature. A spokesperson for the supervisor of elections office confirmed to The Washington Post that the “information [on the site] is accurate.” The decision to vote by mail comes as Trump pushes the Save America Act — a sweeping bill to reform nationwide balloting, including restrictions on mail-in voting — and derides vote-by-mail at events. “Mail-in voting means mail-in cheating,” Trump said yesterday during a law enforcement task force meeting in Memphis. “I call it mail-in cheating, and we’ve got to do something about it all.” The president has long criticized mail-in voting, despite its popularity: About 1 in 3 Americans voted by mail in 2024 . Much of Trump’s antipathy for vote-by-mail stems from his 2020 loss, when he felt overly permissive election laws allowed too many Americans to vote by mail without specific reason. Advertisement Trump did not have to vote by mail. He was in Palm Beach over the weekend, and early in-person voting was available through Sunday. The Supreme Court heard arguments for a case regarding mail-in voting on Monday and appeared likely to embrace a conservative challenge to tallying mail-in ballots that arrive after Election Day. Last year, Trump wrote on social media that he was “going to lead a movement to get rid of MAIL-IN BALLOTS,” and falsely claimed that the United States was the only country that uses the practice. “ELECTIONS CAN NEVER BE HONEST WITH MAIL IN BALLOTS/VOTING,” he wrote. Olivia Wales, a White House spokesperson, said Trump using a mail ballot in the special election was “a non-story” because it’s common knowledge that Trump votes in Florida but lives in Washington, D.C. “As President Trump has said, the Save America Act has commonsense exceptions for Americans to use mail-in ballots for illness, disability, military, or travel — but universal mail-in voting should not be allowed because it’s highly susceptible to fraud,” Wales added. Advertisement The Florida race between Gregory and Maples is an interesting one, in part because of Gregory’s decision to run at this moment. “I’ve been thinking about it for a few years, and just sort of noodling on what has the greatest impact on myself, my neighbors, my family,” Gregory, a mom of three, told us in an interview. “I saw this seat as an opportunity. ... I think it’s a flippable district.” Democrats have overperformed in similar elections since the start of Trump’s second term, consistently outdoing what the district’s makeup would suggest. A recent upset win in a Texas state Senate contest further emboldened Democrats heading into the midterms . Maples, despite initially responding to our request for comment, declined to speak with us for this piece or answer questions about mail-in voting. Maples is running with Trump’s “Complete and Total” endorsement , something the former star basketball player at Palm Beach Atlantic University has called “surreal.” Trump is “the most transformative leader I’ve seen in my lifetime. The positive impacts of his presidency will be felt for generations,” Maples wrote on Instagram in response to the endorsement. Gregory said she has tried to keep her race against Maples focused on local issues, rather than the famous Palm Beach resident, such as schools and affordability. But she is running in a district where, yes, many people know him personally, and his repeated visits affect the quality of life in the area. The president’s presence has changed traffic patterns at Palm Beach International Airport and on the roads around his club. But she added that she would be a representative for Trump as much as for anyone else in the district. “I would fight to lower his property insurance premium,” she said with a laugh when asked about what she could offer the president through local representation. Asked about Trump voting by mail, Gregory noted the ongoing “affordability crisis” and said, “Voting by mail is safe, secure, and a great way to exercise this important right without disrupting their ability to put food on the table and provide for their families.” It’s telling that someone like Gregory is running in a red district at this moment. Why a candidate runs for office is often an underexplored aspect of a political campaign. Sometimes they have been representing the area for years, other times the seat is just the next step up the political ladder. But in wave years — those moments where one political party dominates the momentum — you see first-time candidates throwing their name on the ballot, willing to take a risk because they, too, feel the energy. This was certainly true in 2018, when Democrats, in response to Trump’s first presidential victory, swept into power in the House. And it appears to be true now, as evidenced by polling, focus groups and people like Gregory deciding to take a chance on running for office in challenging districts. “As the backlash against soaring costs intensifies, voters aren’t just holding Republicans accountable at the polls — they’re raising their hand to run for office,” said Heather Williams, president of the Democratic Legislative Campaign Committee. Scientists may have discovered why. What we’re watching Is Donald Trump losing his nerve on Iran? The president announced yesterday that the United States is negotiating with Iran to end the three-week-old war and that he has told the U.S. military to postpone strikes against Iranian energy infrastructure for five days. Trump wrote on social media that the pause would be “SUBJECT TO THE SUCCESS OF THE ONGOING MEETINGS AND DISCUSSIONS.” The president could easily walk away from this pause and continue to strike the nation’s infrastructure, but this moment — the first of its kind since the war began in February — raised questions about whether the byproducts of the conflict, from a slumping stock market to sky high gas prices to concern among parts of his base at home, were beginning to weigh on the Republican leader. The president’s announcement, predictably, sent stock prices soaring, and the markets closed up over 1 percent across the board. The announcement also sent oil futures prices falling over 10 percentage points . Which brings us to the reason for this pause. The Iranian Foreign Ministry swiftly rejected the president’s description of the talks, arguing that Trump was working to “reduce energy prices and gain time to implement his military plans.” And Iranian parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf wrote on X , “No negotiations have been held with the US, and fakenews is used to manipulate the financial and oil markets." Some political watchers accused the president of TACO — the “Trump Always Chickens Out” acronym the president gained during his on-again, off-again implementation of tariffs. “Trump seems, for now at least, to have blinked,” wrote neoconservative commentator Bill Kristol. “This outcome — which I hasten to add, may only be temporary — is still an indictment of his unjustified, reckless and damaging war. But it’s the better path for the nation and the world. All I have to say to the president is, ‘Keep on blinking.’” But an end to the fighting is also what Trump’s opponents have long wanted, so they haven’t been quick to mock the president on the issue. <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/car-ln-tr/471a651/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/17/62/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3>

President Donald Trump, who is pressuring senators to curb the use of mail-in voting, voted by mail in a special election happening today in Palm Beach County, Florida.

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=906529&li=powerup&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3&stpe=default>

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=906529&li=powerup&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3&stpe=default>

That is the news we broke last night in a piece on how Trump uses a mail-in ballot while asking lawmakers to limit the practice <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/car-ln-tr/471a651/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/20/62/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3>.

The Palm Beach County Supervisor of Elections website indicates that Trump, who is registered to vote at his Mar-a-Lago estate on Palm Beach, “voted by Mail Ballot” in a special election between Democrat Emily Gregory and Republican Jon Maples for a seat in the state legislature. A spokesperson for the supervisor of elections office confirmed to The Washington Post that the “information [on the site] is accurate.”

The decision to vote by mail comes as Trump pushes the Save America Act — a sweeping bill to reform nationwide balloting, including restrictions on mail-in voting <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/car-ln-tr/4709f34/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/21/62/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3> — and derides vote-by-mail at events.

“Mail-in voting means mail-in cheating,” Trump said yesterday <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/471a652/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/22/62/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3> during a law enforcement task force meeting in Memphis. “I call it mail-in cheating, and we’ve got to do something about it all.”

The president has long criticized mail-in voting, despite its popularity: About 1 in 3 Americans voted by mail in 2024 <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/471a653/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/23/62/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3>. Much of Trump’s antipathy for vote-by-mail stems from his 2020 loss, when he felt overly permissive election laws allowed too many Americans to vote by mail without specific reason.

Trump did not have to vote by mail. He was in Palm Beach over the weekend, and early in-person voting was available through Sunday. The Supreme Court heard arguments for a case regarding mail-in voting on Monday and appeared likely to embrace a conservative challenge to tallying mail-in ballots <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/car-ln-tr/471a654/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/25/62/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3> that arrive after Election Day.

“ELECTIONS CAN NEVER BE HONEST WITH MAIL IN BALLOTS/VOTING,” he wrote.

Olivia Wales, a White House spokesperson, said Trump using a mail ballot in the special election was “a non-story” because it’s common knowledge that Trump votes in Florida but lives in Washington, D.C.

“As President Trump has said, the Save America Act has commonsense exceptions for Americans to use mail-in ballots for illness, disability, military, or travel — but universal mail-in voting should not be allowed because it’s highly susceptible to fraud,” Wales added.

The Florida race between Gregory and Maples is an interesting one, in part because of Gregory’s decision to run at this moment.

“I’ve been thinking about it for a few years, and just sort of noodling on what has the greatest impact on myself, my neighbors, my family,” Gregory, a mom of three, told us in an interview. “I saw this seat as an opportunity. ... I think it’s a flippable district.”

Democrats have overperformed in similar elections since the start of Trump’s second term, consistently outdoing what the district’s makeup would suggest. A recent upset win in a Texas state Senate contest further emboldened Democrats heading into the midterms <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/car-ln-tr/46ae6bf/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/28/62/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3>.

Maples, despite initially responding to our request for comment, declined to speak with us for this piece or answer questions about mail-in voting. Maples is running with Trump’s “Complete and Total” endorsement <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/471a656/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/29/62/69c266b9b02cb0598c6264b3>, something the former star basketball player at Palm Beach Atlantic University has called “surreal.”

Trump is “the most transformative leader I’ve seen in my lifetime. The positive impacts of his presidency will be felt for generations,” Maples wrote on Instagram in response to the endorsement.

Gregory said she has tried to keep her race against Maples focused on local issues, rather than the famous Palm Beach resident, such as schools and affordability. But she is running in a district where, yes, many people know him personally, and his repeated visits affect the quality of life in the area. The president’s presence has changed traffic patterns at Palm Beach International Airport and on the roads around his club.

But she added that she would be a representative for Trump as much as for anyone else in the district.

“I would fight to lower his property insurance premium,” she said with a laugh when asked about what she could offer the president through local representation.

Asked about Trump voting by mail, Gregory noted the ongoing “affordability crisis” and said, “Voting by mail is safe, secure, and a great way to exercise this important right without disrupting their ability to put food on the table and provide for their families.”

It’s telling that someone like Gregory is running in a red district at this moment.

Why a candidate runs for office is often an underexplored aspect of a political campaign. Sometimes they have been representing the area for years, other times the seat is just the next step up the political ladder. But in wave years — those moments where one political party dominates the momentum — you see first-time candidates throwing their name on the ballot, willing to take a risk because they, too, feel the energy. This was certainly true in 2018, when Democrats, in response to Trump’s first presidential victory, swept into power in the House.

And it appears to be true now, as evidenced by polling, focus groups and people like Gregory deciding to take a chance on running for office in challenging districts.

“As the backlash against soaring costs intensifies, voters aren’t just holding Republicans accountable at the polls — they’re raising their hand to run for office,” said Heather Williams, president of the Democratic Legislative Campaign Committee.
