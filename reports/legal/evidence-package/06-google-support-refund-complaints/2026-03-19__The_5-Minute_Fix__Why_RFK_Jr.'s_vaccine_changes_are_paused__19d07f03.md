Yes, keep this newsletter By Amber Phillips Mainstream public health experts say the federal government’s recent rollback of childhood vaccine recommendations isn’t scientifically sound, polls show it is unpopular , and this week a federal judge said it may be illegal. These are major setbacks for President Donald Trump, who told his vaccine-skeptical health secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. to “go wild” on vaccines. But experts say this may just be the beginning of a long and confusing fight that could ultimately make it harder for parents to keep their kids safe from diseases. “Parents are trying to understand: ‘What is changing, why are things changing and what is best for my child?’” said Jen Kates, the director of global health and HIV policy at KFF, a nonprofit health policy research, polling and news organization. Here’s how public health experts answer those questions. What’s changing: The government recommended less vaccines for kids. A court paused those. In January, the Department of Health and Human Services said vaccines for flu, RSV, rotavirus, hepatitis A and meningitis are no longer recommended for children and parents should consult their doctors. It was a seismic shift in U.S. vaccine policy. The government can’t mandate or ban vaccines but their recommendations have — until now — carried major weight for what doctors recommend, what states require for schools and what insurers approve. But those rollbacks are on hold as of this week. On Monday, a federal judge paused them , saying the government changed vaccine policy after doing away with independent experts. The government skipping over its own process to make vaccine changes, wrote U.S. District Court judge Brian E. Murphy, reflected “strong indication of something more fundamentally problematic: an abandonment of the technical knowledge and expertise embodied by that committee.” The case is taking place in Massachusetts and Murphy was appointed by President Joe Biden. Why things are changing so much right now Kennedy has long argued that looser vaccine recommendations will make kids healthier by avoiding unnecessary medical intervention. When he became the nation’s top health official, he dismissed an influential vaccine panel and filled it with a number of vaccine skeptics . Kennedy and his allies argue they are providing much-needed critical eyes to the U.S. medical system. “This decision protects children, respects families, and rebuilds trust in public health,” he said when the vaccine rollbacks were announced. Advertisement But many experts, including some of the nation’s top health officials appointed by Trump and pushed out by Kennedy, warn that with these rollbacks children could go unprotected from diseases that routinely sicken and kill children. William Schaffner is an infectious disease expert who served on the federal vaccine policy panel for years. He said he’s actually seen less medical rigor as the government rolled back vaccine recommendations. “Questions and skepticism have always been with us,” he said, “those were the hallmarks of the previous vaccine committee. What’s disturbing is that these new committee members have overt biases, many of them.” “I am not sure who the Secretary is listening to, but it is quite certainly not to us," the government’s former top immunization expert, Demetre Daskalakis, wrote when he resigned in August. “Unvetted and conflicted outside organizations seem to be the sources HHS use over the gold standard science of CDC and other reputable sources.” More changes are likely coming The judge’s ruling is temporary, and if the government appeals, this could find its way to the Supreme Court. Advertisement In the meantime, nearly 30 states— including some Republican-led ones — and the nation’s association of pediatricians are recommending people follow the vaccine schedule that was in place before Kennedy, which were based on decades of scientific research. The American Medical Association says it will also review the latest science on vaccines, previously the government’s domain. So essentially, there are dueling authorities on how parents should vaccinate their children. “It puts the onus on the individual family to navigate this,” Kates said. How to keep kids safe amid all the changes Most mainstream public health experts say it’s a bad idea to listen to the federal government when it says to vaccinate less without providing real data on why. Mainstream public health experts say the federal government’s recent rollback of childhood vaccine recommendations isn’t scientifically sound, polls show it is unpopular <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/470df89/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/13/47/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3>, and this week a federal judge said it may be illegal.

These are major setbacks for President Donald Trump, who told his vaccine-skeptical health secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. to “go wild” on vaccines. But experts say this may just be the beginning of a long and confusing fight that could ultimately make it harder for parents to keep their kids safe from diseases.

“Parents are trying to understand: ‘What is changing, why are things changing and what is best for my child?’” said Jen Kates, the director of global health and HIV policy at KFF, a nonprofit health policy research, polling and news organization.

Here’s how public health experts answer those questions.

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=987489&li=thefix&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3&stpe=default>

<https://sli.washingtonpost.com/click?s=987489&li=thefix&m=629a290b99188f8d03d630cf68dfeaec&p=69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3&stpe=default>

What’s changing: The government recommended less vaccines for kids. A court paused those.
In January, the Department of Health and Human Services said vaccines for flu, RSV, rotavirus, hepatitis A and meningitis <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/4658baa/69603defcc447d05b2bbdcdd/596a0e109bbc0f6d71c91171/14/52/69603defcc447d05b2bbdcdd?utm_campaign=wp_the_5_minute_fix&utm_medium=email&utm_source=newsletter> are no longer recommended for children and parents should consult their doctors. It was a seismic shift in U.S. vaccine policy. The government can’t mandate or ban vaccines but their recommendations have — until now — carried major weight for what doctors recommend, what states require for schools and what insurers approve.

But those rollbacks are on hold as of this week. On Monday, a federal judge paused them <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/470df8a/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/17/47/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3>, saying the government changed vaccine policy after doing away with independent experts.

The government skipping over its own process to make vaccine changes, wrote U.S. District Court judge Brian E. Murphy, reflected “strong indication of something more fundamentally problematic: an abandonment of the technical knowledge and expertise embodied by that committee.” The case is taking place in Massachusetts and Murphy was appointed by President Joe Biden.
Why things are changing so much right now
Kennedy has long argued that looser vaccine recommendations will make kids healthier by avoiding unnecessary medical intervention. When he became the nation’s top health official, he dismissed an influential vaccine panel and filled it with a number of vaccine skeptics <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/470df8b/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/18/47/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3>. Kennedy and his allies argue they are providing much-needed critical eyes to the U.S. medical system. “This decision protects children, respects families, and rebuilds trust in public health,” he said when the vaccine rollbacks were announced.

But many experts, including some of the nation’s top health officials appointed by Trump and pushed out by Kennedy, warn <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/44d555d/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/20/47/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3> that with these rollbacks children could go unprotected from diseases that routinely sicken and kill children.

William Schaffner is an infectious disease expert who served on the federal vaccine policy panel for years. He said he’s actually seen less medical rigor as the government rolled back vaccine recommendations. “Questions and skepticism have always been with us,” he said, “those were the hallmarks of the previous vaccine committee. What’s disturbing is that these new committee members have overt biases, many of them.”

“I am not sure who the Secretary is listening to, but it is quite certainly not to us," the government’s former top immunization expert, Demetre Daskalakis, wrote when he resigned in August. “Unvetted and conflicted outside organizations seem to be the sources HHS use over the gold standard science of CDC and other reputable sources.”
More changes are likely coming
The judge’s ruling is temporary, and if the government appeals, this could find its way to the Supreme Court.

In the meantime, nearly 30 states— including some Republican-led ones — and the nation’s association of pediatricians are recommending people follow the vaccine schedule that was in place before Kennedy, which were based on decades of scientific research. The American Medical Association says it will also review <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/46bf322/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/22/47/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3> the latest science on vaccines, previously the government’s domain.

So essentially, there are dueling authorities on how parents should vaccinate their children. “It puts the onus on the individual family to navigate this,” Kates said.
How to keep kids safe amid all the changes
Most mainstream public health experts say it’s a bad idea to listen to the federal government when it  <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/46bf322/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/23/47/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3>says to vaccinate less without providing real data on why.

With vaccine skepticism among parents pronounced especially after the pandemic, measles are already the highest they’ve been in decades and there is an uptick in whooping cough. “These are the first two diseases that are the harbinger of others that will come back if we reduce our vaccinations,” said Schaffner. The U.S. government is warning <https://s2.washingtonpost.com/470df8c/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3/60395ca19bbc0f23b15847a7/24/47/69bc65e0c7565458b7923bd3> that polio is circulating in much of the rest of the world.

“The world is not a big place,” Shaffner said. “What’s over there can be over here really quickly.”
